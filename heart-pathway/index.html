Why this pathway exists

The HEART Pathway isn't a checklist to clear — it's a way of sorting chest pain into four very different futures, fast, without missing the one that's actively killing tissue.

Every patient who comes through the door with chest pain is one of four stories. The whole job of the pathway is to figure out which one — quickly, and without over- or under-calling it:

STORY 1

Occlusion now

  • STEMI or occlusion MI on a non-STEMI ECG
  • An artery is shut. Muscle is dying.
  • Needs reperfusion — minutes matter
STORY 2

ACS, not occluding

  • NSTEMI / unstable angina
  • Real coronary disease, not a full occlusion yet
  • Needs admit, meds, monitored cardiac workup
STORY 3

Truly low risk

  • Story not concerning, ECG clean, trop rules out
  • Won't benefit from admission or more acute testing
  • Safe discharge with follow-up
THE TWO ERRORS

What we're avoiding

  • Missed ACS — sent home, comes back worse
  • Unnecessary admit — beds, cost, downstream testing harm
Why nurses own this

You are the continuity. The provider sees the patient in snapshots; you're at the bedside watching the movie. The pathway's whole premise is that the picture evolves — and the person most likely to catch the turn is the nurse who knows what "the turn" looks like.

House Sup lens

Every one of those four stories is a different bed. The earlier you read which story you're in, the earlier you're working the right placement — discharge home, obs/tele, PCU, ICU, or CVICU. Disposition thinking starts at triage, not at the order.

The science behind ACS

Coronary disease isn't a pipe that slowly clogs until it stops. It's a plaque that tears, clots, and changes by the minute. That's why nothing you check is a single snapshot.

It's a rupture, not a slow clog

The old mental model was a narrowing lumen filling in like a rusty pipe. What actually drives most ACS is a vulnerable plaque in the artery wall that ruptures or erodes. Platelets pile onto the raw surface and form a thrombus. The danger isn't how narrow the artery looked last month — it's whether the cap tore today.

Why this matters at the bedside

This is why a patient can have a "clean cath" or a normal stress test a year ago and still be having a real MI right now. Plaque vulnerability beats plaque size. Nothing risk-stratifies to zero.

Partial vs. complete occlusion

  • Partial / intermittent clot → ischemia that comes and goes. Symptoms wax and wane. ECG can look normal between episodes.
  • Complete occlusion → full-thickness infarction unless flow is restored. This is the one chasing the clock.

And the clot is not static. It can spontaneously reperfuse (the patient feels better, ECG improves) and then re-occlude (pain returns, ECG re-changes). The same artery can open and shut several times in an afternoon.

The one idea to take away

ACS is a moving target. That single sentence explains why we draw serial troponins, repeat ECGs when pain comes back, and don't get falsely reassured by one normal set. You are sampling a process, not reading a verdict.

Why symptoms, ECG, and troponin all evolve

Because the occlusion itself evolves, every downstream marker lags and shifts: pain tracks flow in real time, the ECG changes over minutes to hours as ischemia deepens or resolves, and troponin only rises once enough muscle has been injured — which takes time. Check too early and you can catch all three before they've declared.

Why the ECG matters — and why one isn't enough

The ECG is the single most important early test, and it's also the one most likely to falsely reassure. "No STEMI" is not the same as "no occlusion."

Why 10 minutes

A STAT 12-lead within 10 minutes of arrival exists to catch the patient whose artery is occluded right now, because reperfusion is the most time-sensitive, highest-mortality-benefit decision in all of ACS (aside from aspirin). Time is myocardium. The 10-minute clock is a triage and charge-nurse responsibility as much as a provider one.

"No STEMI" ≠ "no occlusion MI"

STEMI criteria were never designed to diagnose acute occlusion — they're a specific pattern, and they're insensitive early. In real numbers, classic STEMI criteria miss more than a quarter of true occlusions, which then get filed as "NSTEMI." Those missed occlusion MIs carry roughly double the mortality of NSTEMIs without occlusion. So a machine read of "no STEMI" or "nonspecific" is the start of the question, not the end.

What this changes for you

When the read says "nonspecific" but the patient looks and sounds like ACS, that's not a green light — that's the exact patient who needs a repeat tracing and a closer set of eyes. The unimpressive ECG in a concerning patient is the trap.

Why serial ECGs

The earliest signs of occlusion are subtle and dynamic — hyperacute T waves before any ST elevation, ST changes that come and go with reperfusion and re-occlusion. A truly ischemic ECG evolves. You only see evolution if you have more than one tracing. When in doubt, one ECG begets another.

Nurse teaching pearls — ECG
  • Repeat the ECG if pain recurs or worsens, or if the story is concerning despite a clean first tracing. Don't wait to be asked.
  • Compare to old and EMS ECGs when available — a "nonspecific" tracing that's actually new is a different animal.
  • Escalate now for dynamic changes between tracings, new ST depression, deep or new T-wave inversions, hyperacute T waves, or any unstable rhythm.
  • Pair the ECG with the patient: changes + ongoing pain is a louder alarm than either alone.

Why the history matters most

The story carries more diagnostic weight than any single test — and the story is often atypical in exactly the patients we most need to catch.

ACS doesn't read the textbook

The classic crushing substernal pain radiating to the left arm is real, but it's far from universal. Atypical presentations are the rule — not the exception — especially in women, people with diabetes, older adults, and non-white patients. As many as a third of all MIs, and a majority of patients over 75, present with no chest pain at all.

Treat all of these as the same chest-pain story until proven otherwise:

  • Pressure, tightness, squeezing, fullness, burning, "indigestion"
  • Discomfort in the throat, jaw, neck, shoulder, back, or one or both arms
  • Shortness of breath, nausea or vomiting, diaphoresis (cold sweat)
  • Unexplained weakness, fatigue, lightheadedness, or just "not right"

Burning and indigestion-type discomfort carry the same risk of MI as classic pressure. Only purely "stabbing" pain leans the odds down — and even that doesn't rule anything out.

Three "rule-outs" that aren't

"The nitro fixed it, so it's cardiac."  →  No. Nitro relief is unreliable in both directions (it can relieve esophageal spasm too). Response to nitro does not rule ACS in or out.

"The GI cocktail fixed it, so it's reflux."  →  No. Antacids relieve pain in up to a quarter of real MIs. A "GI cocktail" is treatment, never a diagnostic test.

"They had a normal stress test recently."  →  A recent negative stress test does not make today's ACS impossible. Plaques rupture independent of prior stenosis; a meaningful share of these patients still have significant disease.

How nurses score the history

The "History" piece of HEART is a judgment, not a yes/no: slightly suspicious (0), moderately (1), highly (2). Your bedside picture — the way they describe it, how they look, whether it's like a prior event — directly feeds that score. A highly suspicious story (2) should make everyone cautious about calling the patient low risk, even if the total adds up to ≤3.

Troponin: injury marker, not a verdict

High-sensitivity troponin T tells you heart muscle was injured. It does not tell you the injury was a Type 1 MI. The number alone never makes the diagnosis — the pattern does.

Injury vs. infarction

An elevated hs-TnT means myocardial injury. MI is only one cause. Up to 85% of elevated troponins in the ED come from something other than Type 1 MI — heart failure, kidney disease, sepsis, PE, tachyarrhythmia, myocarditis, critical illness. With high-sensitivity assays, up to half of patients without ACS still have a detectable level. So the skill isn't reading "positive/negative" — it's interpreting the value in context and watching the trend.

Why the delta is the whole game

A single value is a still frame. The delta — the change between serial draws — is what separates an acute process from a chronic one.

RISING / FALLING

Abnormal delta

  • > 5 ng/L at 1 hour, or
  • > 7 ng/L at 3 hours
  • A moving troponin = acute injury. In the right story, that's MI until proven otherwise.
FLAT

  • Troponin below sex-specific cutoff, and

Flat-but-elevated vs. rising

A troponin that's elevated but flat across serial draws usually means chronic injury — the failing kidney, the stiff heart, the baseline that's always 40. A troponin that's rising or falling points to something acute. Same number, completely different meaning, depending on whether it moved.

Nurse operational pearl

You protect the delta. Document the exact time the baseline troponin was drawn so the 1-hour or 3-hour repeat lands on time. A delta drawn early, late, or missed entirely is how a real rise gets blurred into "indeterminate." The timing is a nursing save.

When troponin isn't ACS

Troponin is organ-specific, not disease-specific. It says the heart muscle was hurt — never why. On a cardiac floor, most of your bumped troponins aren't plaque ruptures. Here's how to tell strain from a heart attack.

The distinction the whole field hinges on: injury vs. infarction

This is the conceptual core of the Fourth Universal Definition of MI. Myocardial injury is any troponin above the 99th-percentile cutoff — damage, from any cause. Myocardial infarction is injury caused specifically by ischemia. A blood test alone can never diagnose an MI:

Acute injury (troponin rising / falling, ≥1 value > cutoff)
+ clinical evidence of ischemia = Acute MI

"Clinical evidence of ischemia" means at least one of: ischemic symptoms, new ischemic ECG changes, imaging showing new loss of viable muscle or a new wall-motion abnormality, or a coronary thrombus on angiography/autopsy. No ischemia, no MI — even with a sky-high troponin.

The mental flip

For a bumped troponin, stop asking "is it positive?" and start asking three questions in order: Did it move? Is there ischemia? What's their baseline? Those three turn a scary number into a clear story.

At a CV specialty hospital, Types 2, 4, and 5 are everyday life. A troponin bump in a fresh post-CABG heart is expected surgical injury — not a missed coronary event. The skill is holding both truths at once: don't panic at the expected bump, and don't get so used to it that you miss a new, dynamic rise riding on top.

The two patients you'll meet every shift: CHF and CKD

CKD / ESRD — the chronically elevated baseline

Dialysis and advanced-kidney patients almost always run a troponin above the population cutoff. Reduced renal clearance, chronic uremic strain, LVH, and microvascular disease all keep it up. It is valid, not a lab error — and it predicts worse cardiovascular outcomes.

Here's the catch: in these patients the absolute number is nearly meaningless. A flat 60 may be their normal. The signal is the delta off their own baseline — a 60 → 110 with an ischemic story is the alarm, not the 60 itself. Knowing or hunting down their prior baseline is gold.

CHF — strain that can turn acute

A failing heart leaks troponin from chronic wall stress, stretch, and subendocardial ischemia — so a stable elevated level often just reflects the disease. But an acute decompensation can drive a genuine rise/fall through a Type 2 mechanism: more wall stress, tachycardia, hypoxemia, and low output all starve a heart that's already working too hard.

So in CHF, flat-elevated usually means chronic strain; rising/falling means something acute is happening — sometimes a Type 2 MI, occasionally a Type 1 on top. Either way, a moving troponin in a decompensating CHF patient earns attention, not a shrug.

The danger cuts both ways
  • Over-calling: treating every bumped CKD/CHF troponin as an acute MI → unnecessary cath, anticoagulation, and risk.
  • Under-calling: "that's just their kidneys / just their heart failure" → missing a real Type 1 MI layered on top, because the baseline elevation lulled everyone.

The delta plus the clinical story is what cuts through both. A known baseline is the single most useful thing you can put in front of the provider.

Nurse operational pearls — the strained heart
  • Hunt the baseline. Prior admits, dialysis records, the last discharge summary — a known baseline turns an ambiguous number into a clear delta.
  • Push for serial sampling in CHF/CKD. A single value in these patients is nearly useless; the trend is everything.
  • Never "ignore it." Any troponin is worse than none, and higher is worse than lower — even chronic elevation marks a high-risk cardiac patient who deserves eyes on them, not dismissal.

The big mimic: PE & the PERC rule

Chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, syncope, that throat/chest tightness — they overlap between ACS and pulmonary embolism. When PE is on the table but suspicion is low, you'll see providers reach for PERC. Here's what it actually does.

What PERC is — and what it isn't

PERC = Pulmonary Embolism Rule-out Criteria. It is not a PE diagnosis score, and it never tells you someone has a clot. It's a clearance checklist used in one specific situation:

  • It's only used when the provider's gestalt / pretest probability for PE is already low.
  • If all 8 criteria are negative, PE can often be ruled out without a D-dimer or CT — sparing the patient labs, radiation, and time.
  • If any one item is positive, the patient is "PERC positive" — meaning PERC can't rule PE out by itself. That is not a diagnosis of PE.
The pearl that prevents misuse

PERC is a stop rule, not a start rule. It exists to stop unnecessary PE testing in very-low-risk patients. It should never be used to dismiss PE when the story, vitals, hypoxia, syncope, DVT signs, or provider concern point to higher risk.

Don't confuse it with HEART

HEART is additive — you sum points into a risk tier. PERC is all-or-none — every one of the 8 has to be clear to "pass," and a single positive item fails the whole rule. So "PERC positive" isn't a high score; it just means the shortcut doesn't apply here.

The 8 PERC criteria — all must be clear

To be PERC-negative (ruled out), every one of these has to be true:

  • Age < 50Younger than 50
  • HR < 100Not tachycardic
  • SpO₂ ≥ 95%On room air
  • No unilateral leg swellingNo one-sided calf/leg swelling
  • No hemoptysisNot coughing up blood
  • No prior DVT / PENo clot history
  • No recent surgery / traumaNone in the recent window
  • No hormone / estrogen useNo OCPs, HRT, etc.

How to read that

She's not PERC-negative — age alone trips one criterion. That does not make PE likely; it only means PERC can't independently rule it out. From here it's provider judgment: if suspicion for PE stays low, the next step may be a D-dimer or no further PE workup per local pathway. If suspicion is moderate/high, that's a Wells / Geneva → D-dimer → CTPA pathway — not PERC.

Nursing & House Sup operations

Keep PE on your radar alongside ACS when you see:

  • Unexplained tachycardia or hypoxia, pleuritic chest pain, syncope, hemoptysis
  • Unilateral leg swelling, recent surgery or immobility, estrogen/hormone use
  • Prior DVT/PE, active cancer, or pregnancy/postpartum status
What it changes for you
  • If PE concern rises, the patient likely needs additional ED workup before disposition — don't let them drift toward discharge on the ACS track alone.
  • If PE is confirmed or strongly suspected, bed need rides on hemodynamics, O₂ requirement, RV strain, troponin/BNP elevation, the anticoagulation plan, and ICU vs. tele. A large clot with RV strain and a bumped troponin is an ICU conversation, not a tele one.
Why this lives in an ACS module

PE is one reason a troponin rises without a coronary occlusion — RV strain, from the Beyond ACS list. Same overlapping symptoms, completely different treatment. Holding both possibilities open is exactly the anti-anchoring habit this whole pathway is built on.

Triple Rule-Out CTA — the gray-zone anatomy tool

Sometimes the team genuinely can't tell whether stable chest pain is coronary, a clot, or the aorta. Triple Rule-Out is one CT that looks at all three dangerous vascular causes at once.

What it is

A CT angiography protocol that evaluates the three vascular killers of chest pain in a single study:

1

Coronary

  • CAD / ACS coronary anatomy — is there a culprit lesion?
2

Pulmonary

  • Pulmonary embolism — clot in the lungs?
3

Aortic

  • Acute aortic syndrome / dissection — is the aorta torn?

It does not replace clinical judgment, ECG, troponin timing, the HEART score, or PE risk screening — it sits alongside them as one more piece of decision support.

From the ED provider's chair

HEART estimates ACS risk, but it doesn't show anatomy and it says nothing about PE or dissection. In gray-area patients where HEART is neither clearly reassuring nor clearly high-risk, Triple Rule-Out can answer whether there's a dangerous vascular cause visible on imaging before committing to discharge vs. obs/admit.

Who's the gray-zone patient?

TRO is for a selected patient — not everyone with chest pain. Picture someone who is:

  • StableNot crashing
  • No STEMIECG doesn't force the cath lab
  • Troponin not yet diagnosticIndeterminate, no clear delta yet
  • HEART in the middleNot low enough to confidently discharge, not high enough to clearly admit/cath
  • Overlapping symptomsChest pressure, throat/jaw/back discomfort, SOB, a pleuritic component, palpitations, syncope-like spells, unexplained tachycardia
  • PE/aorta still plausibleAge or risk factors keep the clot and the aorta on the table

Where TRO does not fit

Not the right tool when…
  • Obvious STEMI / occlusion MI needing the cath lab — go to cath, not CT.
  • Hemodynamic instability or shock — too unstable for the scanner.
  • Severe hypoxia or obvious high-risk PE — target it directly.
  • A strong classic dissection story — that's a dedicated aortic CTA + urgent surgical pathway, now.
  • A clear diagnosis where a targeted, focused study images it better.
  • As a blanket test for every chest-pain patient — it's a selected-use tool, not a default.

The one line to remember

Triple Rule-Out is a gray-zone anatomy tool. HEART estimates ACS risk. PERC/Wells estimate PE risk. TRO helps when the provider is asking: is there a dangerous vascular cause we can see — coronary, PE, or aorta — before deciding discharge vs. obs/admit?

The research words, in plain English

None of these are as scary as they sound — they're shorthand researchers use. Here's what each one actually means at the bedside, and why it matters for the patient in front of you.

The "is this number abnormal?" words

99th percentile / Upper Reference Limit (URL)

The line between "normal" and "abnormal" for troponin.

Picture this: line up 100 perfectly healthy people by their troponin, lowest to highest. The 99th percentile is the value 99 of them fall under. Cross that line and you're outside what almost every healthy heart shows.

Why you care It's a statistical "normal ceiling," not a magic heart-attack number. Above it = injury worth explaining — but it never tells you the cause. That's still the story's job.

ng/L (nanograms per liter)

Just the measuring stick — how much troponin is in the blood.

Scale check: a nanogram is a billionth of a gram. We're measuring vanishingly tiny amounts, which is exactly why the assay has to be so precise.

Why you care Every cutoff you use — 14, 22, 52 — is in this one unit. "18 ng/L" vs "52 ng/L" is just less or more troponin on the same ruler.

Delta (Δ)

The change between two troponin draws. "Δ" is just the Greek letter for "difference."

That's the whole trick: baseline 18, repeat 27 → Δ +9. The delta is simply the movement between draws.

Why you care A moving troponin = acute. A flat one = chronic. The delta is the most useful thing on the entire trend — which is why you guard the timing of the draws.

High-sensitivity assay

A newer test that can "see" much lower troponin levels than the old ones.

Like an upgrade: from reading glasses to a microscope. You catch tiny amounts of troponin earlier — sometimes within an hour of injury.

Why you care Sharper eyes are why we can rule in or out faster with 1-hr and 3-hr deltas. It's also why up to half of people without ACS now show a detectable number — the test picks up normal wear-and-tear too.

The "can we trust the machine?" words

Coefficient of variation (CV)

How much the result wobbles if you ran the exact same sample over and over.

Like a bathroom scale: step on it five times. Reads 150, 150, 151, 150, 149 → low wobble, trustworthy. Reads 150, 162, 144 → you can't trust a small change. A good troponin assay wobbles ≤10%.

Why you care Low wobble is what lets us trust a small real change (a Δ of +6) instead of writing it off as noise. The precision is the whole reason the delta means something.

Limit of blank / detection / quantitation (LoB / LoD / LoQ)

Three steps in "how low can the test reliably see?"

Like hearing a whisper across a room:  LoB = the background hum when no one's talking.  LoD = "I can tell someone whispered" (there's some troponin).  LoQ = "I can tell you exactly what they said" (a number you can trust).

Why you care This is why a result can read "undetectable" (<6 ng/L) — the test genuinely sees nothing — which in the right very-low-risk patient is reassuring on its own.

The "how good is this test?" words — sensitivity & specificity

Every test can fail two ways: it can miss a real case (false negative) or it can cry wolf (false positive). These two words just tell you which failure a test is good at avoiding.

Sensitivity

Catches real cases. Miss-proof.

A sensitive test rarely misses someone who truly has the disease (few false negatives) — like a smoke detector that goes off for every fire.

SnNout → a Sensitive test, when Negative, rules it OUT.
Specificity

Avoids false alarms. Picky.

A specific test rarely flags someone who's actually fine (few false positives) — like a strict bouncer who only stops real troublemakers.

SpPin → a Specific test, when Positive, rules it IN.
See it in the pathway

This is exactly why "no STEMI" can't reassure you: STEMI criteria are insensitive for occlusion — they miss a lot of real ones. And it's why a positive modified Sgarbossa (99% specific) gets believed: when something that picky fires, it's almost always real.

PPV & NPV (predictive values)

When the test is positive — or negative — how much should you actually believe it?

NPV = when it says "negative," how often that's truly right. The HEART pathway's 99.6% NPV means: of the low-risk patients it ruled out, 99.6% really had no MI or death at 30 days. That's the number that lets us send them home. PPV is the same idea for a positive result.

Why you care The twist: these depend on who you test. The same result means different things in a high-suspicion vs. low-suspicion patient. That's the math behind "the story matters" — and why we don't order troponin on people with no real suspicion for ACS.

Likelihood ratio (+LR / −LR)

How much one finding should move your suspicion up or down.

Needle-mover: a big +LR shoves you toward the disease; a tiny −LR pulls you away. A high-risk HEART score has a +LR around 13 (strongly toward ACS); a low-risk HEART has a −LR around 0.20 (strongly away).

Why you care You don't need the math. Just know some findings barely move the needle, and some — a highly suspicious history, a dynamic ECG — move it hard. That's why those get the override.

The whole tab in one breath

Sensitive → trust it when it's negative (SnNout). Specific → trust it when it's positive (SpPin). 99th percentile = the healthy "normal ceiling." Δ = the change. NPV = how much you can trust a negative. Everything else is detail.

The override rule

HEART supports judgment — it never replaces it. A highly suspicious history (2) or acute ischemic ECG changes (2) should keep a patient out of the low-risk bin regardless of total score. Discharging someone with high clinical suspicion just because the math says ≤3 is exactly the failure mode this pathway is built to prevent.

  • Cardiac historyKnown CAD, prior stents, CABG, prior MI — raises the floor
  • Pending cardiologyConsult ordered? Seen? Plan documented yet?
  • What's still pendingDocument open items before any disposition is called
Translate risk into a bed

The House Sup question underneath all of it: where does this patient sleep? The ladder at our shop:

  • Low risk, ruled out → discharge home — clear the projected admit early.
  • Intermediate / indeterminate → obs or tele — plan the monitored bed before the bottleneck.
  • High risk / unstable / dynamic ECG / rising trop → PCU or ICU — PCU for higher-acuity monitoring; ICU once there are drips that need titrating (nitro, heparin, pressors, antiarrhythmics).
  • CVICU → reserved for the patient headed to emergent CABG or needing mechanical circulatory support (MCS) — IABP, Impella, ECMO.

Start the placement the moment the picture turns, not after the order.

Why it's a flow issue, not just a clinical one

At a 22-bed cardiovascular specialty hospital, the difference between a tele bed, a PCU bed, an ICU bed for titratable drips, and a CVICU bed held for emergent surgery or MCS is a real constraint on the board. Reading the trajectory early — rising trop, recurrent pain, new instability — is also reading the next four hours of bed demand. The clinical call and the capacity call are the same call.

What could go wrong

Every one of these is a near-miss that has happened in real EDs. Knowing the shape of the error is how you catch it.

The patient who looks stable but is evolving

Comfortable now, vitals fine, first set unremarkable — and the artery re-occludes an hour later. The fix: serial sampling and a low threshold to repeat the ECG when something changes, not on a fixed timer alone.

"No STEMI" read as "all clear"

A non-STEMI ECG in a concerning patient is the missed-occlusion trap. STEMI criteria miss a quarter of occlusions. The unimpressive tracing in a sick-sounding story earns more scrutiny, not less.

Troponin drawn too early — or the delta missed

A 0-hr drawn 30 minutes into symptoms can be normal before the rise. If the repeat is late or skipped, the delta — the whole diagnostic signal — disappears. Protect the timing.

Ongoing symptoms that never get escalated

The patient quietly mentions the pain came back, it gets charted, and nothing happens. Recurrent or persistent pain is an active alarm — repeat ECG, tell the provider, reassess the plan.

Anchoring on GI / anxiety / musculoskeletal too early

"Reflux," "panic attack," "pulled muscle" — comfortable labels that close the workup prematurely. Cardiac ischemia mimics all three. Keep ACS on the list until it's actually ruled out — and remember PE wears the same clothes (pleuritic pain, SOB, palpitations, syncope), so it stays on the list too.

HEART score used without judgment

The most dangerous one: trusting a ≤3 in a patient whose story screams cardiac. The score is a tool. The patient is the data. When they disagree, believe the patient and escalate.

Case: the "almost low risk" patient

A walkthrough of the exact patient this pathway is built to catch — atypical, borderline on paper, and dynamic once you watch the trend.

Presentation. 58-year-old woman, history of type 2 diabetes and hypertension, arrives with ~2 hours of "bad indigestion," mild shortness of breath, and feeling washed out. No classic chest pain. She's diaphoretic but says she "probably just ate something." BP 138/84, HR 92, SpO₂ 96%. First ECG: nonspecific, no STEMI.

T = 0 · TRIAGE

Score the story honestly

Diabetic woman, atypical-but-classic-for-atypical presentation, diaphoresis, exertional fatigue. This is moderately-to-highly suspicious history, not reassuring. Early HEART estimate: H1 · E0 · A1 · R2 · T?

T = 0 · FIRST TROPONIN

Baseline hs-TnT = 18 ng/L (indeterminate)

Above the female cutoff of 14, below 52 — squarely in the gray zone. Adds 1 point. Running HEART ≈ 5 (intermediate). Document the exact draw time so the 1-hr delta lands on schedule.

T = 1 HR · DELTA + SYMPTOMS

Repeat hs-TnT = 27 ng/L → delta +9 ng/L

That's > 5 ng/L at 1 hour — an abnormal, rising delta. The troponin moved: this is acute injury, not a chronic baseline. Around the same time she says the "indigestion" is back. Repeat the ECG now.

DISPOSITION DECISION

What tips discharge → admit

Three things move her out of any "low-risk discharge" conversation: a suspicious story, a rising delta, and recurrent symptoms. Whatever the first total looked like, this is not a rule-out. Cardiology involved; admit for monitored cardiac care, serial trop/ECG continuing, urgent invasive eval per cards.

Nursing watch-outs in this case
  • Don't let "indigestion" + female + diabetic anchor the team to GI — that's the population that presents atypically.
  • You owned the delta timing, so the +9 rise was unmistakable instead of blurred.
  • Recurrent symptoms triggered a repeat ECG without waiting to be told.
  • Keep continuous monitoring on — rising trop + recurrent pain is the window where rhythm or hemodynamic instability shows up.
House Sup bed planning

At triage she read as a possible discharge or short obs. The moment the delta rose and pain recurred, the placement shifted to a monitored tele or PCU bed with cardiology — stepping up to ICU if she needs titratable drips (nitro, heparin), and CVICU only if she destabilizes toward MCS or emergent surgical revascularization. Working that bed at T+1hr — not at the admit order — is what keeps the board ahead of the patient.

Bedside teaching script

A 3–5 minute version to say out loud at huddle or the bedside. Spoken language, not slides — the bolded lines are the ones to land.

"Reading the story before it ends" — huddle script

Open · 20 sec

"Quick one on chest pain. The HEART Pathway isn't a box to check — it's how we sort one symptom into four totally different patients, fast, without missing the one whose artery is actually shutting. Our job as nurses is to catch the turn."

The big idea · 40 sec

"Here's the one thing to remember: ACS is a moving target. A plaque tears, it clots, and that clot can open and close several times in an afternoon. That's why one normal ECG and one normal troponin don't close the case — we're sampling a process, not reading a verdict."

ECG · 45 sec

"On the ECG — 'no STEMI' is not 'all clear.' STEMI criteria miss more than a quarter of real occlusions, and those carry double the mortality. So if the read says 'nonspecific' but the patient looks like ACS, that's our cue to repeat the tracing and grab a closer look — especially if the pain comes back."

History · 40 sec

"The story matters more than any single test, and it's atypical in exactly the people we most need to catch — women, diabetics, older folks. Pressure, indigestion, jaw or arm, short of breath, just 'not right.' And three traps: nitro working, the GI cocktail working, or a recent normal stress test — none of those rule out ACS."

Troponin · 40 sec

"Troponin means injury — not automatically a heart attack. The magic is the delta. A number that's elevated but flat is usually chronic. A number that's rising — more than 5 at an hour — is acute. So the most valuable thing you'll do is document the exact time you drew the baseline, so the repeat lands on time and we can actually see the trend."

Close · 30 sec

"Bottom line: the score supports our judgment, it doesn't override it. If the story screams cardiac, a low number doesn't send them home. And every risk level is a different bed — discharge, tele, or CVICU. The earlier we read the story, the earlier we've got the right bed ready. Questions?"

ACS HEART Pathway — Bedside Quick Reference

CV Nursing Teaching Series · the why behind the pathway, in one page

The one idea

ACS is a moving target — plaque tears, clots, opens, re-closes. One normal set ≠ ruled out. You're sampling a process.

HEART = H · E · A · R · T

  • History 0/1/2 (slight/mod/high)
  • ECG 0/1/2
  • Age <45 / 45–64 / ≥65
  • Risk factors 0 / 1–2 / ≥3 or known dz
  • Troponin normal / 1–3× / >3×

Abnormal delta: >5 ng/L @1hr or >7 ng/L @3hr = acute.  Rule-out: HEART ≤3 + below cutoff + delta <3 @1hr (or ≤5 @3hr). Flat-elevated = usually chronic.

Disposition

  • 0–3 Low → d/c (only if story not high, ECG clean, no recurrent sx, trop ruled out)
  • 4–6 Intermediate → obs/tele, serial trop+ECG, cards
  • 7–10 High → admit, cards, urgent invasive eval

Escalate immediately

  • Recurrent / persistent chest pain
  • Dynamic ECG, new ST depression, hyperacute/new TWI
  • Hypotension, new O₂ need, CHF signs
  • New arrhythmia / palpitations

Three "rule-outs" that aren't

  • Nitro relief — not a rule-in/out
  • GI cocktail relief — not a rule-in/out
  • Recent normal stress test — not protective today

Nurse saves

  • Chart exact baseline trop time → protect the delta
  • Pain back → repeat ECG, don't wait
  • Atypical = women, diabetics, elderly, non-white
  • Read risk early → ready the right bed

Troponin up ≠ always MI

Injury vs. infarction

  • Injury = trop > cutoff (any cause)
  • MI = injury + ischemia (symptoms / new ECG / wall-motion / thrombus)
  • Type 2 = supply–demand (sepsis, anemia, HTN, shock, tachy)
  • Type 4/5 = post-PCI / post-CABG — expected bump

CHF & CKD patients

  • Chronically elevated baseline is real, not false +
  • Absolute number ≈ useless — read the delta off their baseline
  • Flat = chronic strain · rising/falling + ischemia = acute on top
  • Hunt the baseline; push serial draws; never "just their kidneys"

Three lenses: HEART = ACS risk · PERC/Wells = PE risk · Triple Rule-Out CTA = gray-zone CT for coronary / PE / aorta when the three can't be separated clinically (stable patients only).

Term key: Sensitive test → trust a NEGATIVE (SnNout) · Specific test → trust a POSITIVE (SpPin) · 99th %ile = healthy "normal ceiling" · Δ = change between draws · NPV = how much you can trust a negative result.

Override rule: the score supports judgment, never replaces it. A highly suspicious history (2) or acute ischemic ECG keeps a patient out of "low risk" regardless of total. When the patient and the score disagree, believe the patient and escalate.
