Advanced Hemodynamic Monitoring: From Signal to Bedside Action

A practical guide to using HemoSphere Alta advanced features to recognize risk, identify the dominant mechanism, test a hypothesis, track a therapeutic target, and document the physiologic response.

Scope

Education and pilot evaluation only. This guide does not replace the current manufacturer instructions for use, institutional policy, provider orders, waveform review, clinical examination, echocardiography, or invasive confirmation when indicated.

The bedside sequence

Use the monitor to answer one question at a time

1 · ValidateIs the signal and clinical setting trustworthy?
2 · DetectHPI: is hypotension risk rising?
3 · DiagnosePreload, contractility, or afterload pattern?
4 · TestFRT: does SV/CO rise with a reversible challenge?
5 · Treat & trackAFM for fluid learning; GDT for time in target.
6 · QuantifyDerived values: delivery, power, work, resistance.
Alarm ≠ diagnosis. Responsiveness ≠ indication. A target ≠ a treatment.
Gate zero

Before trusting an algorithm

  • Confirm the arterial waveform, pressure bag, leveling, zero, and sensor connection.
  • Confirm rhythm, ventilation, position, and the technology supplying each value.
  • Pause interpretation during flush/ABG draw, repositioning, surgical manipulation, or acute line artifact.
  • Record recent fluid, bleeding, ventilation, vasopressor, inotrope, and vasodilator changes.
  • Prefer a coherent trend across MAP, SVI/CI, SVV/PPV, SVR, oxygen delivery, exam, and echo—not one tile.
Preload

Is there recruitable stroke volume?

Use a reversible test when possible. Static filling pressure alone is not proof that fluid will help.

Contractility

Is flow low despite an adequate tank?

Trend SVI/CI and dP/dt in context. Confirm unexpected or high-risk patterns with echo and/or PAC data.

Afterload

Is the ventricle pushing against the wrong load?

Pair MAP with SVR/SVRI and SVI/CI. A normal pressure can hide low flow behind excessive resistance.

Detect + diagnose

Hypotension Predictive Index (HPI)

Best moment: continuous supported monitoring when early recognition could create time to reassess physiology before MAP falls.
  1. Confirm a valid supported sensor and arterial waveform.
  2. Open Side Panel → Hypotension Predictive Index.
  3. When HPI rises, verify the trend and current MAP; do not treat the score alone.
  4. Open the secondary relationship screen and review preload, contractility, and afterload together.
  5. Check recent events: vasodilation, fluid loss, position, ventilation, pacing/rhythm, inotrope or pressor changes.
  6. Form a mechanism-first plan and reassess the response.

Manual screen guide

Screens reproduced from the supplied HemoSphere Alta 1.6 operator manual, pp. 201-202.

Manufacturer behavior: HPI is displayed 0–100 and updates every 20 seconds; the manual describes a high alert above 85 after two consecutive readings. A low HPI does not exclude later hypotension.

Do not: convert HPI ≥85 into an automatic fluid bolus or pressor order. Evidence is strongest in monitored perioperative populations; ICU/post-cardiotomy use still requires clinical validation.

Test

Fluid Responsiveness Test (FRT)

Best moment: when a fluid decision is uncertain and a reversible preload challenge is safe.
  1. Open Side Panel → Fluid Responsiveness Test.
  2. Select Passive Leg Raise or Fluid Bolus, the connected technology, and SV/SVI/CO/CI.
  3. Choose the test duration supported by the technology.
  4. Obtain the required stable baseline with the patient still.
  5. Perform the challenge exactly as selected; for PLR, move from semi-recumbent to supine with legs at 45° promptly.
  6. Interpret the change in flow with BP, congestion risk, and the clinical reason for giving—or withholding—fluid.

Manual screen guide

Screens reproduced from the supplied operator manual, pp. 256-258.

Key distinction: a positive test means flow is preload-responsive; it does not prove that fluid is necessary, safe, or the best treatment.

Pause or qualify: rapidly changing pressors, unstable rhythm, active manipulation, severe RV failure, altered intrathoracic pressure, or an unreliable flow signal.

Treat + learn

Assisted Fluid Management (AFM)

Best moment: a supported radial arterial waveform case with valid dynamic conditions, a defined fluid strategy, and a need to learn from serial bolus responses.
  1. Zero the Acumen IQ sensor and verify waveform quality.
  2. Open Side Panel → Assisted Fluid Management.
  3. Select the correct surgery mode and fluid strategy (10%, 15%, or 20%).
  4. Enter the Maximum Case Volume as a guide, then initialize.
  5. Review each suggestion; accept or decline based on the full clinical picture.
  6. If accepted, start User Bolus, give the intended bolus, stop it promptly, and enter the actual volume/time.
  7. Review the analyzed change in SV/SVV before the next decision.

Manual screen guide

Screens reproduced from the supplied operator manual, pp. 238-242.

Strategy meaning: 10% is more liberal and 20% more restrictive; the percentage is the expected SV change normalized to 500 mL, not a mandatory 500 mL bolus.

Known confounders: inaccurate flow, acute vasoactive change, repositioning, ventilator change, surgery/manipulation, arterial-line interference, concurrent unrecorded fluid, or bleeding at/above the infusion rate.

Track

Goal Directed Therapy (GDT)

Best moment: after the team has selected patient-specific endpoints and wants to measure whether therapy keeps them in range over time.
  1. Choose the key parameters on the trend screen.
  2. Open Side Panel → Goal Directed Therapy.
  3. Edit individualized target ranges; avoid copy-pasting generic targets into every lesion or shock state.
  4. Select the parameters and press Play.
  5. Use Time-in-Target and the trend shape—not a single green value—to judge stability.
  6. Annotate interventions and review prior sessions under Events & Intervention.

Manual screen guide

Screens reproduced from the supplied operator manual, pp. 253-254.

Useful combinations: MAP + SVI/CI; SVI + SVRI; CI + DO₂I; or lesion-specific endpoints during a defined phase of care.

Do not: optimize a number while tissue perfusion, congestion, ischemia, rhythm, or the operative problem is worsening.

Quantify + document

Derived Value Calculator

Best moment: when the team needs a calculated snapshot to answer a specific question or compare serial phases.
  1. Open Side Panel → Derived Value Calculator.
  2. Select the desired variable and enter the required source values carefully.
  3. Confirm units, body-surface indexing, sample timing, and source reliability.
  4. Interpret the result with its components; do not let a calculated number conceal a bad input.
  5. Use Log Values to preserve the snapshot under Events & Intervention.

Manual workflow excerpt

Excerpt reproduced from the supplied operator manual, p. 90.

High-value calculations: CPO/CPI, DO₂/DO₂I, VO₂/VO₂I, SVR/SVRI, PVR/PVRI, LVSWI, RVSWI, and indexed stroke volume.

Remember: the calculator is not a continuous trend unless values are logged serially. Calculated precision cannot exceed input precision.

Events & Intervention log

This is the connective tissue between the features. Mark fluid, pressor, inotrope, vasodilator, rhythm/pacing, ventilation, position, procedure, and bleeding events so the trend has a clinical story.

A value without time + intervention + response is only a number.

Dynamic preload measures: validity checklist

  • Controlled mechanical ventilation and adequate tidal excursion
  • Stable rhythm without major beat-to-beat irregularity
  • Closed-chest and relatively stable intrathoracic/abdominal pressure conditions
  • No major spontaneous respiratory effort
  • Reliable arterial waveform and flow calculation
  • No concurrent maneuver that changes preload, afterload, or contractility

Manufacturer-specific AFM requirement: the supplied manual states accurate SVV for AFM requires mechanical ventilation with tidal volume ≥8 mL/kg. Apply current device instructions and local lung-protective ventilation policy.

Common cognitive traps

“HPI is high, so give fluid.”

Wrong shortcut. HPI signals risk; the relationship screen and clinical context help determine whether the mechanism is preload, contractility, afterload, or an external event.

“SVV is high, therefore the patient is dry.”

SVV can be elevated or uninterpretable from ventilation, rhythm, RV dysfunction, abdominal pressure, open chest, and waveform problems. Even valid responsiveness does not establish net benefit from fluid.

“MAP is normal, so output is fine.”

MAP can be preserved by high SVR despite low SVI/CI. Pair pressure with flow and delivery.

“dP/dt is a direct contractility meter.”

It is a loading-condition-sensitive arterial waveform trend, not a substitute for ventricular imaging or invasive confirmation.

“The calculated value is objective.”

The formula may be objective; the input timing, units, sampling, and measurement quality may not be.

Stop and escalate

Signal failure

New damping, artifact, implausible values, or sensor disagreement.

Clinical discordance

Monitor suggests improvement while exam, perfusion, congestion, lactate, ECG, or echo worsens.

Time-critical cause

Tamponade, bleeding, ischemia, graft/valve failure, PE, tension physiology, or MCS/device complication.

How this module was developed

  1. Extracted the device-defined steps, supported technologies, thresholds, warnings, and confounders from the supplied operator manual.
  2. Separated what the device does from what the clinician should infer.
  3. Mapped each feature to a physiologic question: risk, mechanism, responsiveness, target tracking, or calculated reserve/delivery.
  4. Cross-checked interpretation against shock-monitoring and fluid-responsiveness consensus literature.
  5. Linked the workflow to existing CVICU teaching cases and added explicit stop rules for conditions requiring echo, PAC, surgical evaluation, or urgent source control.

Pilot intent: use structured clinician feedback to assess clarity, bedside realism, perceived usefulness, unsafe ambiguity, and cases where the monitor and clinician disagree. This is not yet a validated clinical protocol.
