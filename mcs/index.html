1 · Start with the job of the device

Do not memorize machines first. Ask what circulation problem the team is trying to solve: augment pressure, unload the LV, replace forward flow, oxygenate blood, support the RV, or buy time while the heart recovers or a destination therapy is decided.

First question

What is failing?

LV, RV, both ventricles, oxygenation, or the whole cardiopulmonary system?

Second question

What is the device replacing?

Pressure, flow, oxygenation, ventricular work, or some combination.

Third question

What remains native?

The patient still has a heart, vasculature, preload, afterload, rhythm and pharmacology. MCS changes the system; it does not erase it.

2 · Impella: think “LV in → aorta out”

The Impella CP draws blood from the LV through the inlet and expels it into the ascending aorta. At the bedside, separate three things: device support, native cardiac contribution, and vascular tone.

Device support

P-level → pump speed → device flow

Higher performance levels generally provide more pump support, but displayed flow is an estimate and must be interpreted with pressure signals, motor current, loading conditions, position, alarms and the patient.

Native heart

Ao and LV are a relationship

Look at the placement signal and LV pressure together. Ask whether native pulsatility is strong, weak, changing, or uncoupled from the prior pattern.

System

Preload still matters

An axial-flow pump cannot move blood that does not reach the LV. Abrupt loss of filling can appear as reduced flow, motor-current change, suction alarms, hypotension or all of the above.

5 · VA ECMO: think “drain → pump → oxygenate → return”

VA ECMO can restore systemic perfusion and gas exchange, but it changes ventricular loading. The bedside nurse should be able to trace the circuit physically and explain what each segment is doing.

Flow side

RPM is not flow

Flow depends on pump speed plus preload/drainage and afterload/resistance. A changing flow at the same RPM is physiologic information.

Gas side

Sweep and FdO₂

Separate CO₂ removal from oxygen transfer. Trace which knob changes which gas-exchange problem according to your circuit and local protocol.

VA-specific

Watch the LV

Peripheral VA ECMO can increase LV afterload. Keep pulsatility, pulmonary edema, LV distention and aortic-valve opening in the mental model.

6 · Troubleshooting: classify before you touch

When the device changes, avoid reflexively changing settings. First identify whether the problem is patient, preload/drainage, position/cannula, pump, circuit/gas, or monitoring. Escalate immediately for instability and follow device-specific institutional policy.

Impella: suction / low-flow pattern

Think low LV filling, RV failure, hypovolemia, tamponade, malposition, excessive support relative to filling, or abrupt hemodynamic change. Correlate the waveform, motor current, alarms, exam and echo.

ECMO: drainage insufficiency

Think venous return limitation, hypovolemia, cannula position, increased intrathoracic pressure, obstruction or excessive RPM relative to available drainage.

ECMO: gas / membrane problem

Differentiate gas-source failure, sweep failure and membrane-lung dysfunction using pre/post-oxygenator assessment and your circuit protocol.

Any MCS: sudden instability

Patient first: rhythm, blood pressure, bleeding, tamponade, access/cannula integrity, disconnection, limb perfusion, neurologic change. Call the appropriate MCS/ECMO team early.

7 · Learning rounds: what to collect for later study

For an orientee who is not assigned to the MCS patient, the goal is disciplined observation without disrupting care or capturing PHI.

Device snapshot

Record the device type, support level, flow, waveforms, purge/circuit data and active alarms. If photography is permitted, avoid identifiers and follow institutional policy.

Drip snapshot

Write down pressors, inotropes, vasodilators, sedation, anticoagulation and major recent changes. Ask: what problem is each drug solving that the machine is not?

Hemodynamic snapshot

HR, rhythm, MAP, filling pressures, CI/CO, SvO₂, lactate, urine output and the direction of travel.

One causal question

“Why is this patient on P-7 and still on vasopressin?”
“Why did ECMO flow fall when RPM did not?”
“What changed before the suction alarm?”

Best home-study method: reconstruct the case as a timeline: patient state → device state → drips → intervention → response. That turns a collection of numbers into physiology.

8 · References & guardrails

Use the device manufacturer’s current Instructions for Use, your institution’s MCS/ECMO policy and competency pathway, and current ELSO guidance for ECMO. This module is a teaching supplement, not an operating manual or substitute for credentialed supervision.

Clinical safety: device settings, anticoagulation, purge management, cannula manipulation, alarm overrides and emergent circuit interventions must follow local policy, provider/MCS-team direction and device-specific training.
