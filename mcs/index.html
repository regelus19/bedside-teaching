1 · Start with the job of the device

Do not memorize machines first. Ask what circulation problem the team is trying to solve: augment pressure, unload the LV, replace forward flow, oxygenate blood, support the RV, or buy time while the heart recovers or a destination therapy is decided.

First question

What is failing?

LV, RV, both ventricles, oxygenation, or the whole cardiopulmonary system?

Second question

What is the device replacing?

Pressure, flow, oxygenation, ventricular work, or some combination.

Third question

What remains native?

The patient still has a heart, vasculature, preload, afterload, rhythm and pharmacology. MCS changes the system; it does not erase it.

2 · Impella: think “LV in → aorta out”

The Impella CP draws blood from the LV through the inlet and expels it into the ascending aorta. At the bedside, separate three things: device support, native cardiac contribution, and vascular tone.

Device support

P-level → pump speed → device flow

Higher performance levels generally provide more pump support, but displayed flow is an estimate and must be interpreted with pressure signals, motor current, loading conditions, position, alarms and the patient.

Native heart

Ao and LV are a relationship

Look at the placement signal and LV pressure together. Ask whether native pulsatility is strong, weak, changing, or uncoupled from the prior pattern.

System

Preload still matters

An axial-flow pump cannot move blood that does not reach the LV. Abrupt loss of filling can appear as reduced flow, motor-current change, suction alarms, hypotension or all of the above.

5 · VA ECMO: think “drain → pump → oxygenate → return”

VA ECMO can restore systemic perfusion and gas exchange, but it changes ventricular loading. The bedside nurse should be able to trace the circuit physically and explain what each segment is doing.

Flow side

RPM is not flow

Flow depends on pump speed plus preload/drainage and afterload/resistance. A changing flow at the same RPM is physiologic information.

Gas side

Sweep and FdO₂

Separate CO₂ removal from oxygen transfer. Trace which knob changes which gas-exchange problem according to your circuit and local protocol.

VA-specific

Watch the LV

Peripheral VA ECMO can increase LV afterload. Keep pulsatility, pulmonary edema, LV distention and aortic-valve opening in the mental model.

Distal perfusion is not “set and forget.” For peripheral femoral VA-ECMO, trend both legs using the local neurovascular/NIRS protocol. New asymmetry, coolness, color change, pain, sensory/motor change, or loss of signal is an urgent escalation.

Differential hypoxemia: find the mixing problem

Pattern

Improving native LV ejection plus poor pulmonary oxygenation can deliver desaturated blood to the coronaries and brain while the lower body receives well-oxygenated ECMO return.

Recognition

Trend the right radial arterial saturation/ABG when configured for peripheral femoral VA-ECMO, plus cerebral oximetry when used. A lower-limb value cannot rule out upper-body hypoxemia.

Response

Escalate to the ECMO team; verify oxygen delivery and gas source, optimize native-lung support, and address circuit/configuration or flow only through the local ECMO plan.

7 · VA-ECMO + Impella: support and unload are different jobs

VA-ECMO supports systemic circulation and gas exchange but may increase LV afterload. An Impella can provide active LV-to-aorta unloading. The combination is often called ECPELLA or ECMELLA; it adds capability and another complete device risk profile.

VA-ECMO adds

Systemic flow

Venous drainage → oxygenation → arterial return

Potential consequence

LV pressure load

Aortic counterpressure may reduce ejection in a severely weak LV

Look for

Distention

Poor AV opening/pulsatility, rising left-sided pressures, pulmonary edema, echo changes

Unloading strategy

Team decision

Impella is one option; venting strategy is individualized

Two preload systems

ECMO needs venous drainage; Impella needs LV filling. A flow change in one device can alter the loading conditions of the other.

Two access/circuit systems

Assess every cannula and access site, distal perfusion, device position, hemolysis, bleeding, anticoagulation exposure, and infection risk.

Never chase a single number

Balance systemic perfusion, LV decompression, aortic-valve opening, pulmonary status, right-heart function, and end-organ response with the MCS/ECMO team.

9 · Weaning is a readiness question, then a supervised trial

No single EF, CI, lactate, or blood-pressure threshold proves recovery. Device-specific weaning and decannulation require a trained team, imaging/hemodynamic integration, and an explicit rescue plan.

Protocol trial

Across the trial

Trend device flow/support, arterial pressure and pulse pressure, rhythm, filling pressures, echo, vasoactives, gas exchange, lactate and end-organ response.

Stop / escalate pattern

New hypotension, escalating vasoactives, worsening filling pressures or RV/LV function, pulmonary edema, falling oxygen delivery, arrhythmia, rising lactate, or device/circuit instability.

After removal

Continue structured hemodynamic and access-site surveillance. Watch for recurrent shock, bleeding, thrombosis, limb ischemia, and the loss of the device’s prior loading effect.

10 · Complications: recognize the pattern, protect the patient

When something changes, classify it before touching settings: patient, preload/drainage, position/cannula, pump, circuit/gas, access/limb, blood, or monitoring. Instability requires immediate escalation and the device emergency pathway.

Impella suction / low-flow

Think low LV filling, RV failure, hypovolemia, tamponade, malposition, excessive support relative to filling, or an abrupt hemodynamic change. Correlate alarms, signal, motor current, exam, and echo.

ECMO drainage insufficiency

Think venous return limitation, hypovolemia, cannula position, increased intrathoracic pressure, obstruction, or RPM beyond available drainage.

Gas / membrane concern

Separate gas-source or sweep failure from membrane-lung dysfunction. Use pre/post-oxygenator gases, circuit pressures, clot inspection, and the local oxygenator-failure pathway.

Hemolysis pattern

Dark urine or rising plasma-free hemoglobin/LDH with anemia, kidney injury, or device changes demands prompt evaluation for suction, position, thrombosis, speed/loading mismatch, or circuit issues.

Bleeding / thrombosis

Trend all access, cannula, chest-tube, neurologic, laboratory, and circuit findings. Anticoagulation is individualized—never import a web target into active care.

Any sudden instability

Patient first: rhythm, pressure, bleeding/tamponade, oxygen delivery, cannula/device integrity, limb perfusion, neurologic change. Call the MCS/ECMO team early.

Bedside surveillance loop: patient perfusion → native heart → device/circuit → access and limbs → blood/anticoagulation → infection → trends and alarms → repeat. A normal device number does not cancel an abnormal patient.

11 · Learning rounds: collect a physiology timeline

For an orientee who is not assigned to the MCS patient, the goal is disciplined observation without disrupting care or capturing PHI.

Device snapshot

Record device/configuration, support level, flow, waveforms, purge/circuit data, and alarms. Follow institutional privacy and photography policy.

Drip snapshot

Pressors, inotropes, vasodilators, sedation, anticoagulation, and major recent changes. What problem is each drug solving that the machine is not?

Hemodynamic snapshot

Rhythm, MAP/pulse pressure, filling pressures, CO/CI when used, saturation, lactate, urine, exam, and direction of travel.

One causal question

Why did flow change at the same setting?
What changed before the suction alarm?
Is perfusion improving—or only the screen?

Reconstruct: patient state → device state → drips → intervention → response. That turns isolated numbers into bedside physiology.
