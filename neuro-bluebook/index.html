The most important skill is not memorization

A Neuro ICU orientation is not a race to memorize every diagnosis. The core competency is recognizing acute deviation from the patient's neuro baseline and acting quickly. Serial assessment, comparison with the prior exam, and timely notification of abnormal findings are central to preventing injury.[1]

Four framing points

01 · Sources of deterioration

Neuro ICU patients can deteriorate from the primary injury, from secondary injury, or from treatment-related complications. Bedside nursing surveillance is often the earliest detector of change.[8]

02 · An exam is not a score

A neuro assessment is a structured synthesis of arousal, cognition and language, cranial nerve clues, motor findings, respiratory pattern, and vital sign context — not a single number.[1]

03 · Trends over values

Trends matter more than isolated values. Document what changed, when it changed, and whether the change is reproducible.[1]

04 · Local policy governs

Verify local policy for neuro check frequency, pupillometry use, sedation interruptions, transport monitoring, and escalation pathways.[8]

Section 03

First shift survival guide

The first 15 to 30 minutes with a new Neuro ICU patient set up the rest of the shift. Assessment at shift change — ideally a joint exam with the outgoing nurse during handoff — establishes and validates the baseline you will compare against all night.[1]

Timeline caution

The sequence below is a workflow, not a timed protocol. Ordered neuro check frequency, sedation plans, and target parameters vary by diagnosis, procedure, and provider plan.[4] Verify local policy

Before report Chart review
  • Diagnosis and reason for Neuro ICU admission, plus the key procedure and its date.[8]
  • Latest imaging — what was seen, when, and whether anything is pending. CT, CTA, MRI, angiography, or EEG results in the next few hours may change care.[8]
  • Neuro baseline — the best known reproducible exam, including any pre-existing deficit.[1]
  • Ordered targets — neuro check frequency, blood pressure goal, sodium goal if ordered, temperature plan, sedation target, ventilation goals if specified, device and drain orders. Diagnosis-specific targets vary and must not be generalized.[4]
  • Airway and ventilation — secured or not, current support, and how tenuous it is.
  • Drains and devices — what is in place and what orders govern it. Verify local policy
  • Infusions — sedation, analgesia, vasoactive agents, and anything titrated to a neuro or hemodynamic target.[3]
  • Pending procedures — anything that will move the patient, interrupt sedation, or change the exam.
Time-sensitive risks to identify before you walk in

Unsecured aneurysm concern, recent thrombolytic or thrombectomy, active ICP issue, seizure concern, recent craniotomy, spinal cord instability, or a tenuous airway.[5]

At the bedside First 15–30 minutes
  1. Verify patient identity per your institution's two-identifier standard. Verify local policy
  2. Perform and document a reproducible baseline neuro exam before nonessential tasks.[1] A joint exam with the off-going nurse is the strongest way to validate it.[1]
  3. Verify pupil findings — size, symmetry, reactivity. If there is asymmetry, establish whether it is known and documented as baseline.[1]
  4. Verify drain level and status against the order. Verify local policy or provider-ordered parameters
  5. Trace every line and infusion from bag to pump to patient, and confirm what each one is titrated to.[3]
  6. Confirm the ordered blood pressure goal and how well the patient has been staying inside it.[5]
  7. Confirm ordered ICP and CPP goals if applicable, stated as ordered goals rather than universal numbers.[8] Verify provider-ordered parameters
  8. Verify emergency equipment at the bedside per unit standard. Verify local policy
  9. Confirm seizure precautions and whether there is EEG monitoring or a seizure concern in the record.[7,9]

First-shift “don't miss” checks

Is the exam even valid?

Ask whether the patient is actually assessable, or whether the exam is confounded by sedation, paralytic effect, metabolic derangement, deafness, aphasia, intubation, or a baseline neurologic deficit.[3]

Are the pupils equal and reactive?

And if not — is the asymmetry known, explained, and documented as baseline?[1]

Anything new?

New motor asymmetry, new inability to follow commands, new language abnormality, or loss of airway protection.[1]

Do the vitals suggest secondary injury?

Fever, hypoxia, hypotension, severe hypertension, or a late Cushing pattern — hypertension with widened pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations.[1]

During the shift Hour by hour
  • Trend rather than document isolated values. Repeating the same fields over time surfaces deterioration patterns earlier than free-text charting alone.[8]
  • Compare every exam with baseline, not only with the immediately previous hour.[1]
  • Escalate acute change promptly using the ordered or unit pathway.[1]
  • Reassess after every intervention and validate suspicious changes by repeating the exam and checking confounders.[1]
  • Track total drain output where a drain is present. Verify local policy
  • Track sodium and fluid balance where a sodium goal is ordered. Verify provider-ordered parameters
  • Prepare early for transport and imaging — monitoring requirements during transport are a local-policy item.[8] Verify local policy
End of shift Hand it off intact
  • Summarize trends, not just the last set of numbers.[1]
  • Reconcile totals — intake, output, drain output where applicable.
  • Name unresolved concerns explicitly rather than leaving them implied.
  • Clarify pending imaging and procedures and what result would change the plan.[8]
  • Give a structured handoff, ideally with a shared bedside exam to validate the baseline for the next nurse.[1] See

Neuroanatomy refresher

Structure predicts deficit. Once you can name what a region does, the bedside finding stops being a fact to memorise and becomes something you can reason to — and reason backward from when a patient changes.

How to read this section

Every card follows the same shape: structure → what it does → what happens when it fails → what you see at the bedside. The chains are for orientation, not diagnosis — nurses do not localise lesions, but nurses are the ones who notice that something changed.

Clinical gem

Aphasia and confusion are charted interchangeably and they are not the same thing. Confusion is a content problem — the patient is disoriented. Aphasia is a channel problem — the patient may be fully oriented and unable to prove it to you. Test comprehension separately from expression before you chart either.[1]

Dominance

Same-size stroke, two completely different patients

Dominant hemisphere — left in the large majority of people
language centres (Broca, Wernicke) live here → left-sided injury → aphasia → deficit is loud and obvious → presents early

Non-dominant hemisphere — usually right
spatial attention lives here → right-sided injury → left neglect, anosognosia → patient does not know anything is wrong → presents late, and often falls

Fall risk

A patient who genuinely does not believe they are weak is a different fall risk from one who forgets to call. Neglect is not non-compliance.

Below the cortex

Deep structures

Small regions, outsized consequences

Thalamus

Relay station for nearly all sensory traffic to the cortex, and a contributor to arousal. Injury causes contralateral sensory loss and can markedly depress consciousness out of proportion to lesion size.

Hypothalamus

Temperature, fluid balance, endocrine control via the pituitary. Injury shows up as temperature instability and sodium and water disturbances — which then feed straight back into cerebral oedema.[14]

Basal ganglia

Movement modulation. A common site of hypertensive intracerebral haemorrhage.[5]

Cerebellum

Coordination, balance, fine motor control — deficits are ipsilateral, unlike the cortex. Sits in the tight posterior fossa, so swelling there compresses the brainstem quickly and can obstruct cerebrospinal fluid outflow.

Posterior fossa

There is very little room back there. A cerebellar bleed or infarct that would be survivable elsewhere can compress the fourth ventricle and the brainstem, causing hydrocephalus and rapid deterioration. Treat any new posterior-fossa symptom — ataxia, severe vertigo, new occipital headache, vomiting — as urgent.[1]

Brainstem

Three levels, and the reflexes that map to each

The brainstem carries every ascending and descending tract, houses most cranial nerve nuclei, and runs the reticular activating system that keeps a patient awake. It is also the structure herniation compresses.

LevelKey rolesBedside proxy
MidbrainCranial nerves III and IV; pupillary reflex; arousal pathwaysPupil size and reactivity — the reason a new sluggish or blown pupil is an emergency[1]
PonsCranial nerves V to VIII; corneal reflex; respiratory rhythmCorneal reflex; abnormal breathing patterns
MedullaCranial nerves IX to XII; cough and gag; cardiac and respiratory centresCough and gag — airway protection, and the reason it is assessed before extubation[1]

Why herniation kills from the bottom up
rising pressure above → tissue forced downward → midbrain compressed first → pupil changes → pons → corneal reflex lost, breathing pattern degrades → medulla → loss of cough, gag, and cardiorespiratory control

Clinical gem

The reflexes you check every hour are a rough map of brainstem level. Losing them in a top-down sequence — pupil, then corneal, then cough and gag — is not a collection of unrelated findings. It is a descent, and the sequence itself is the escalation.[1]

Why the deficit is on the other side

Decussation and the homunculus

Two ideas that explain most stroke patterns

Crossing
motor fibres descend from cortex → cross at the medulla → left hemisphere controls right body → lesion side and weakness side are opposite

The exception worth knowing: the cerebellum does not cross this way. Cerebellar findings are on the same side as the lesion.

The homunculus
body is mapped along the motor strip → face and hand near the lateral surface → leg and foot tucked over the top into the midline → which artery is blocked predicts which body part is weak

Clinical gem

This is why “face and arm worse than leg” and “leg worse than arm” are meaningful phrases rather than incidental detail — they point at different vessels. Chart the pattern, not just “left-sided weakness.”[1]

Circle of Willis

The anterior and posterior circulations join in a ring at the base of the brain, allowing collateral flow when one vessel is compromised — which is part of why two patients with the same occlusion can look different. The ring is also where berry aneurysms form, at the branch points, which is why aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage clusters there.[6]

Clinical gem

Posterior circulation strokes do not look like the poster. No facial droop, no arm drift — instead dizziness, imbalance, double vision, or a patient who is intermittently “just off.” The BE in BE-FAST exists precisely because these were being missed.[12]

Cerebrospinal fluid

Made, circulated, absorbed — and what happens when the loop breaks

Normal circuit
choroid plexus in the lateral ventricles → foramen of Monro → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle → subarachnoid space → absorbed into venous circulation

Obstructive hydrocephalus
blood, mass, or oedema blocks a narrow point → fluid keeps being produced upstream → ventricles enlarge → pressure rises → declining consciousness, headache, vomiting → drainage may be required[2]

Communicating hydrocephalus
subarachnoid blood impairs reabsorption → production continues, clearance fails → pressure rises → a recognised complication after subarachnoid haemorrhage[6]

Why the drain sits where it does

An external ventricular drain enters a lateral ventricle and is levelled to a reference approximating the foramen of Monro — the anatomy above is the reason that landmark, and not some arbitrary point, is the one that matters.[2]

Cerebral blood flow

Autoregulation, and what happens after it is lost

A healthy brain holds its own blood flow steady across a wide range of blood pressures by constricting and dilating its vessels. Injured brain frequently loses that ability — and once it does, cerebral blood flow follows systemic blood pressure passively.

Autoregulation lost
blood pressure drops → cerebral blood flow drops with it → ischaemia → oedema → pressure rises → perfusion falls further

And in the other direction
blood pressure surges → flow rises unopposed → hyperaemia, oedema, or haematoma expansion[5]

Why hypotension is treated so aggressively here

A single hypotensive episode after traumatic brain injury is independently associated with worse outcome.[13] In an injured brain, a blood pressure that would be unremarkable on a medical floor can be an ischaemic event. Blood pressure goals are individualised and provider-ordered. Verify ordered parameters

Carbon dioxide is a cerebral vasodilator
rising carbon dioxide → cerebral vessels dilate → more blood volume in a fixed box → pressure rises
falling carbon dioxide → vessels constrict → pressure falls, but so does perfusion → short-term rescue only[14]

Clinical gem

This is why a plugged endotracheal tube, a fighting patient, or an under-supported respiratory rate is a neurological problem and not only a respiratory one. Carbon dioxide is one of the fastest levers on intracranial pressure that exists, and it moves in both directions.[14]

Section 05

Neuro assessment

For awake patients, the baseline focused assessment includes level of consciousness, language and speech, cranial nerve findings relevant to bedside nursing, motor movement and sensation, and vital signs. For patients with altered consciousness, the focused exam emphasizes level of consciousness, pupils and eye findings, protective reflexes, motor response, and vital signs.[1]

The documentation standard

Document a structured narrative plus standardized fields — not only “neuro intact” and not only a GCS number. A change in level of consciousness is the most sensitive indicator of a neurologic problem.[1]

What to assess

Arousal on a described scale — alert, drowsy, obtunded, stuporous, comatose — and the stimulus required: spontaneous, voice, touch, or pain.[1]

What trends matter

A change in level of consciousness is described as the most sensitive indicator of a neurologic problem. Track the stimulus needed over time, not just the label.[1]

When to escalate

New decrease in level of consciousness or inability to arouse.[1]

Orientation, cognition, and mental status
What to assess

Orientation, attention, comprehension, and ability to follow commands. Subtle changes in memory, affect, mood, attention, and personality may be early clues.[1]

What trends matter

Whether speech and language abnormalities reflect aphasia, dysarthria, delirium, or sedation — these move differently over time.[1,3]

When to escalate

New loss of command following not explained by sedation or a metabolic cause.[1]

Speech and language Aphasia vs dysarthria
What to assess

Fluent or nonfluent output; naming, repetition, and comprehension when feasible.[1]

What trends matter

Distinguish inability to speak because of intubation from aphasia or encephalopathy, and chart which one you mean.[1]

When to escalate

New aphasia or dysarthria — either may signal stroke progression or new cranial nerve dysfunction.[1]

Glasgow Coma Scale Trend the components

The GCS remains useful for describing arousal and response pattern, especially in patients with reduced consciousness, but it does not replace a full neuro exam. GCS and the FOUR score are identified as useful tools for evaluating level of consciousness, with the emphasis on serial structured assessment beyond the score itself.[1]

What to assess

Eye, verbal, and motor response as three separate observations.[1]

What trends matter

Trend the component scores, not only the total. A drop in eye, verbal, or motor response can matter even when the total decline looks small. In intubated patients, clearly document the verbal limitation rather than implying a normal verbal response.[1]

When to escalate

Acute decline in GCS or in any component score — especially with pupillary change, motor asymmetry, or an abnormal respiratory pattern.[1]

Pupils Fastest deterioration check

Pupillary assessment is a core bedside cranial nerve screen and one of the most important rapid checks for deterioration. Pupil size and reactivity are essential findings. Medication effects may alter size and reactivity, but new asymmetry not explained by medication may indicate brainstem injury, increased ICP, or possible herniation.[1]

What to assess & document
  • Right pupil size and reactivity
  • Left pupil size and reactivity
  • Symmetry or anisocoria
  • Pupillometer values if used under local policy
What trends matter

Note the trend rather than a single isolated number — this applies to pupillometer readings as well.[1] Verify local policy on pupillometry and escalation thresholds

When to escalate
  • New unilateral fixed or sluggish pupil
  • Rapidly worsening asymmetry from baseline
  • Pupillary change with decreased LOC, vomiting, posturing, new weakness, or an abrupt hypertension and bradycardia pattern[1]
Gaze, extraocular movement, and facial symmetry

For bedside nursing, the most relevant cranial nerve observations are vision and visual fields when feasible, pupils, extraocular movements and gaze, facial symmetry, corneal protection in low-LOC patients, and swallow and cough protection.[1]

What to assess

Gaze position at rest, extraocular movements when assessable, any diplopia complaint, facial symmetry, and dysarthria.[1]

What trends matter

New gaze deviation, a new diplopia complaint, or abnormal extraocular movement may suggest focal or brainstem involvement. Facial droop or dysarthria may signal stroke progression or new cranial nerve dysfunction.[1]

When to escalate

New gaze deviation, new facial droop, or new dysarthria.[1]

Protection, not just localization

Loss of cough or gag, or inability to manage secretions, raises aspiration and airway risk. Absent corneal protection in low-LOC patients increases eye injury risk and requires eye protection measures per local practice.[1] Verify local policy

Motor strength, drift, and posturing

Motor assessment should prioritize symmetry, command following, antigravity movement, drift, and meaningful response to stimulation in less responsive patients. Focus on right-versus-left comparison, resistance testing when possible, spontaneous movement, and describing abnormal flexion or extension in comatose patients.[1]

Standardized language

Follows commands in all extremities → localizes → withdraws → abnormal flexion → extension → no response. Chart right arm, left arm, right leg, and left leg separately.[1]

What trends matter

Describe new pronator drift, new hemiparesis, new flaccidity, or new posturing explicitly rather than labeling the patient “weaker.”[1]

When to escalate
  • New focal weakness
  • Loss of antigravity movement
  • Loss of command following not explained by sedation or metabolic cause
  • New posturing or asymmetry to painful stimulus[1]
On strength grading scales

Numeric strength grading criteria are not reproduced here; the Batch 1 evidence package specifies symmetry, antigravity movement, drift, and resistance testing as the bedside priorities. Use your institution's documentation scale.[1] Verify local policy

What to assess

Sensory findings when the patient is assessable.[1]

What trends matter

Right-versus-left comparison, and whether a reported change is reproducible on repeat testing.[1]

When to escalate

Treat a new reproducible sensory change as part of a focal neurologic change and escalate with the rest of the exam.[1]

Sedation, delirium, and agitation as exam context

Routine ICU assessment of sedation and agitation, and screening for delirium with validated tools, is part of standard adult ICU care.[3]

  • Use a sedation scale such as RASS consistently for ICU patients receiving sedating medications.[10,11]
  • Use the delirium screening tool your unit uses — commonly CAM-ICU — when the patient is arousable enough for the screen to be valid.[3] Verify local policy on delirium screening workflow and validity
  • The neuro exam is unreliable on intravenous sedating agents. Assessment is more reliable if sedatives are titrated or held according to the ordered plan — but only if the medical condition permits, and only within unit protocol and patient-specific safety constraints.[1] Verify local policy on sedation interruption and contraindications
Do not assume agitation is behavioral

In the Neuro ICU, “agitated” may represent pain, delirium, hypoxia, urinary retention, rising ICP, nonconvulsive seizure activity, or worsening hemorrhage.[3]

Baseline versus acute change

The baseline neuro exam is the best known reproducible neurological assessment used as the reference point for later comparison.[1] Every hourly exam is a comparison against two things: the last documented exam, and that baseline.[1]

  • Establish it early. Perform and document a reproducible baseline before nonessential tasks; validate it with a joint exam at handoff when possible.[1]
  • Validate suspicious change. Reassess, check confounders, and ask whether the change is reproducible.[1]
  • Cluster beats single findings. Treat clustered change as higher risk than one isolated finding — for example, increasing somnolence plus new pupillary asymmetry plus rising blood pressure.[5]
Documentation tips
  • Chart actual findings, not “unchanged,” unless your documentation system already captures a validated baseline elsewhere.[1] Verify documentation expectations
  • Distinguish “unable to assess” from “normal.”[1]
  • Use dedicated notation for confounders: sedation bolus, paralytic exposure, transport, imaging, procedure, or post-ictal state.[3]
  • Document what changed, when it changed, and whether the change is reproducible.[1]
  • Chart each extremity separately; describe posturing as abnormal flexion or extension rather than shorthand.[1]
  • For intubated patients, state the verbal limitation explicitly.[1]

The stimulus ladder

Escalate in order — do not start with pain

  1. Voice. Speak normally, then loudly. Many patients charted as unresponsive were simply not addressed loudly enough.[1]
  2. Touch. Light touch or gentle shaking of the shoulder.
  3. Central stimulation. Only if there is no response to voice or touch. Central sites test brain response — trapezius squeeze and supraorbital pressure are the standard choices.[1]
  4. Peripheral stimulation. Nail bed pressure tests a spinal reflex arc and can produce movement in a patient with no cerebral response, so it cannot be used alone to score motor.[1]
Skin safety

Repeated sternal rubs cause skin breakdown and bruising. Rotate the site, use the minimum stimulus that answers the question, and document which site was used so the next nurse can reproduce your exam.[1]

Clinical gem

Localising means the patient purposefully reaches toward the stimulus — crossing midline or moving above the clavicle toward a supraorbital stimulus. Withdrawal pulls away from it. That single distinction is the most commonly miscounted point on the whole scale, and it is the one that changes the motor score.[1]

Posturing

Flexion versus extension, at a glance

Arms flexed and drawn in toward the core, wrists and fingers flexed, legs extended. The mnemonic is arms toward the core. Indicates injury above the level of the midbrain.[1]

Decerebrate — abnormal extension (M2)

Arms extended and internally rotated, wrists pronated, legs extended. Indicates injury at or below the midbrain and is the more ominous of the two.[1]

Escalate

New posturing of either type, or a change from flexion to extension, is an immediate escalation finding — do not wait for the next scheduled check.[1]

NIH Stroke Scale — bedside administration

What it is and when you will perform it

A standardised, serial measure of stroke deficit

The NIHSS is an internationally recognised tool used to monitor, trend, and compare neurological deficits following stroke. Its value is in reproducibility — the same items, scored the same way, by every clinician, every time.[12]

When it is performed
  • Baseline on admission to a stroke or neuro unit
  • At the frequency the orders specify
  • With any significant change in neurological status
  • Before and after intervention, including thrombolysis and thrombectomy
The eleven domains

Level of consciousness with questions and commands, best gaze, visual fields, facial palsy, motor arm (left and right), motor leg (left and right), limb ataxia, sensory, language, dysarthria, and extinction or inattention.[12]

What matters at the bedside

A rising score means worsening deficit. Any increase from the previous score — particularly in a patient after thrombolysis or thrombectomy — is escalated immediately, not deferred to the next scheduled assessment.[12]

Frequency of stroke-specific assessments and required documentation are institution-specific. Verify local policy

Administering it without invalidating it

The rules that keep serial scores comparable

  1. Score what you see on the first attempt. Do not coach, do not repeat until the patient improves, and do not score what you believe they could do.[12]
  2. Follow the item order. The scale is designed to be administered in sequence; jumping around changes what later items are measuring.[12]
  3. Untestable items have defined handling. Amputation, joint fusion, intubation and similar situations have specific scoring conventions — use them rather than skipping the item or guessing.[12]
  4. Use the certified materials. Score the picture, naming and reading cards your institution certifies you on, in the current version. Do not substitute your own.[12]
  5. Score the deficit, not the cause. The scale measures what the patient can do right now; it does not ask you to explain why.[12]
Clinical gem

The score is only useful as a trend, and a trend only holds if the method holds. If you and the previous nurse administered it differently, the difference between the two scores is measuring the two of you, not the patient. When a score changes unexpectedly, repeat it yourself before escalating — then escalate on what you find.[12]

Monro-Kellie doctrine — why one extra millilitre matters

The fixed box

Three compartments, one total that cannot change

The adult skull is a rigid container with a fixed internal volume. Three things live inside it, and after the fontanelles close the sum of the three cannot grow.[13]

Brain tissue · roughly 80%

Parenchyma plus interstitial water. Grows with cytotoxic or vasogenic oedema, tumour, or abscess.[14]

Blood · roughly 10%

Arterial and venous. Rises with hypercapnia, hypoxaemia, obstructed venous outflow, seizure, pain, agitation, or fever.[13]

CSF · roughly 10%

Produced and absorbed continuously. Accumulates when outflow is obstructed — intraventricular blood, subarachnoid blood, or mass effect on the ventricles.[2]

The rule

If any one compartment grows, another must shrink or intracranial pressure rises. There is no fourth option.

Compensation, then the cliff

The brain buys time, and then it runs out

Compensation is real but finite. The two movable compartments — CSF and venous blood — are displaced first, which is why a patient can be accumulating a serious mass while the ICP still reads normal.[14]

Compensated — ICP still normal
mass or oedema begins → CSF shifts to the spinal subarachnoid space → venous blood compressed out → total volume held constant → ICP normal, exam unchanged

Decompensated — ICP climbing
displacement reserve exhausted → small added volume → steep ICP rise → CPP falls (CPP = MAP − ICP) → cerebral blood flow drops → ischaemia → more oedema → ICP rises further

Herniation — the only direction left is down
pressure exceeds what the compartments can hold → tissue shifts across dural folds or the foramen magnum → brainstem compression → loss of pupillary, respiratory and haemodynamic control

Why this spirals

Ischaemia causes swelling and swelling causes more ischaemia. Once that loop closes it feeds itself, which is why early, small interventions beat aggressive late ones.[13]

The volume-pressure curve, in words

Compliance is what you assess; ICP is only where it happens to be sitting

Plot added volume along the bottom and ICP up the side. The line is nearly flat at first, then bends sharply upward. Two consequences matter at the bedside.

  1. A normal ICP does not mean a safe brain. A patient sitting at the end of the flat portion looks fine and has no reserve left.[14]
  2. Late on the curve, tiny volumes cause large pressure jumps. Suctioning, a coughing fit, a flexed neck, a flat bed, a tight tracheostomy tie.[13]
Clinical gem

Two patients can both sit at an ICP of 14. The one whose ICP spikes to 30 with turning and takes five minutes to settle has poor compliance. The one who blips to 18 and recovers in seconds does not. Chart the recovery, not just the peak — a pressure that no longer returns to baseline after routine care is a change worth escalating.[1]

Stroke

Two mechanisms, one shared consequence

Both end in inadequate cerebral perfusion

Ischaemic

A blocked artery — thrombus or embolus — interrupts blood flow to a territory of brain. Treatment is aimed at restoring flow: thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy, with eligibility determined by the treating team.[12]

Haemorrhagic

Rupture of a weakened vessel causes bleeding and limits perfusion. Treatment is aimed at stopping the bleeding and limiting expansion: blood pressure control to the ordered goal, reversal of anticoagulation where indicated, and surgical options.[5]

The branch point

Non-contrast head computed tomography answers one question first — is there blood? The answer sends the patient down two very different pathways, and the interventions are close to opposite. Never assume the pathway before imaging is resulted.[12]

Levelling

The reference point, and why it decides everything

An EVD drains by pressure exerted from the brain and by gravity, which means the height of the system relative to the patient determines how much drains. The transducer and the drainage chamber are levelled to an anatomical reference approximating the foramen of Monro — the external auditory meatus is the landmark in common use.[2]

Why levelling is not a formality
transducer too low → system reads falsely high, drains too much → risk of over-drainage
transducer too high → system reads falsely low, drains too little → missed intracranial hypertension

Re-level after every position change

Any bed adjustment, turn, transfer, or change in head-of-bed angle invalidates the previous level. Re-level and re-zero per policy before trusting the next number, and confirm whether the drain should be open, clamped, or set to pressure monitoring against the current order.[2] Verify local policy

Clinical gem

CPP = MAP − ICP. If the transducer is off, both halves of that equation are wrong — and every titration decision made from it is wrong too. Levelling is not housekeeping; it is the measurement.[2]

Reading the waveform

Three peaks, and what their shape tells you about compliance

P1 — percussion wave

Arterial pulsation. Normally the tallest of the three.

P2 — tidal wave

Reflects brain compliance. This is the peak that changes shape as compliance worsens.

P3 — dicrotic wave

Follows aortic valve closure. Normally the smallest.

Normal — “descending stairs”
P1 taller than P2 → P2 taller than P3 → good compliance

Abnormal — “mountain” waveform
P2 rises to meet or exceed P1 → rounded, peaked appearance → poor compliance → the brain will not tolerate any added volume — blood, CSF, or oedema

Abnormal — dampened waveform
flattened, low-amplitude trace → suspect an equipment problem first → kinked tubing, clot, air, transducer or stopcock position, disconnection → also seen where a bone flap has been removed

Read the waveform before you believe the number

A dampened trace means the displayed ICP may not be real. Troubleshoot the system, confirm the stopcock is in the ordered position, and escalate rather than charting a reassuring number you cannot trust.[2]

Medical therapy for raised ICP and for bleeding

The organising principle

Every therapy below is aimed at one of three things

What the drips are trying to do
pull water out of brain tissue · stop or limit bleeding · prevent secondary injury

Secondary injury is the damage that happens after the initial event — from hypotension, hypoxaemia, fever, seizure, and pressure. Preventing it is the whole point of the intensive care unit stay.[13]

Hyperosmolar therapy

Raising serum osmolality to draw water out of the brain

Hypertonic sodium solutions

Directly add sodium, raising serum osmolality and drawing water from brain tissue into the vascular space. Also expand intravascular volume, which supports blood pressure. Recommended over mannitol for initial management of elevated ICP or cerebral oedema in traumatic brain injury.[14]

Concentration, route, central versus peripheral access, and administration rate vary by preparation and institution. Verify concentration and access standards

Mannitol

An osmotic diuretic. Raises serum osmolality and produces a diuresis, so volume status must be watched alongside the neurological effect. An effective alternative where hypertonic sodium is contraindicated.[14]

Administered through a filter per manufacturer instruction; renal function is monitored after administration.[14] Verify ordered parameters

What the nurse monitors
  • Serum sodium and osmolality against the ordered goal — the goal is set by the provider, not by a general reference[14]
  • Volume status, urine output, and blood pressure — the two agents pull in opposite directions here
  • Renal function, particularly with repeated mannitol dosing[14]
  • The neurological exam — the number that matters most is whether the patient is better
Clinical gem

Avoid hyponatraemia in any patient with severe neurological injury — a falling sodium worsens cerebral oedema, and it can undo in an hour what osmotic therapy spent a shift achieving. Hypotonic intravenous fluids have no place here.[14]

Stopping the bleed

Reversal of anticoagulation

In intracranial haemorrhage, identifying and reversing a contributing anticoagulant is time-critical. Confirm the patient's medication history and check the relevant coagulation studies before assuming which agent is involved — the reversal strategy depends entirely on the answer.[5]

Time to reversal drives outcome. The fastest thing a nurse can contribute is an accurate medication history with the last dose time, drawn labs, and established access — often before the reversal order is written.[5]

Head of bed elevated, head midline, neck neutral. Elevating the head of the bed is a recognised adjunct for reducing ICP.[14]

Section 10

Neuro emergencies

Escalation is organized around change from baseline plus the associated risk pattern — not around memorizing a separate algorithm for every diagnosis. Use your institutional stroke, seizure, ICP, and rapid response pathways.[8]

Activation criteria are not universal

Stroke alert activation criteria, rapid response versus direct provider escalation, and EEG or seizure alert workflow are local-policy items. This page describes bedside actions and reporting, not who to call at your hospital.[8]

Primary algorithm

Acute neuro change

  1. Recognize the change from baseline. Compare against the last documented exam and the best known baseline.[1]
  2. Stay with the patient. Confirm the change yourself before acting on someone else's description of it.[1]
  3. Assess airway, breathing, circulation. Airway protection and ventilatory support are the first things at risk.[1]
  4. Check glucose. Dysglycemia is one of the reversible threats to screen for immediately.[5]
  5. Reassess pupils and motor exam. Size, symmetry, reactivity; command following, antigravity movement, symmetry, posturing.[1]
  6. Review sedation and paralytics. Recent bolus, current infusion, paralytic exposure, and whether the exam can be interpreted at all.[1,3]
  7. Verify drains, tubing, transducers, and positioning against the order. Verify local policy or provider-ordered parameters
  8. Confirm blood pressure and oxygenation against the ordered goal. Hypoxia, hypotension, and severe hypertension worsen secondary injury.[1,5]
  9. Notify the appropriate provider or activate the emergency response using the ordered or unit pathway. Communicate the exact neuro change, the associated vitals, and the suspected triggers.[1]
  10. Prepare for the likely next steps as ordered — CT, CTA, EEG, airway management, osmotic therapy, seizure treatment, labs, or bedside provider assessment, depending on diagnosis-specific protocol.[9]
  11. Document time, findings, interventions, and response. Report last-known-normal, baseline, associated vital and device trends, recent procedures or medication changes, and the actions already taken.[1]

Situation guides

Each guide below follows the same order: confirm, screen reversible threats, escalate, prepare. Where the Batch 1 package does not support device- or electrolyte-specific management, that is stated rather than filled in.

Sudden pupil change High risk
Confirm

Recheck both pupils for size, symmetry, and reactivity. Establish whether the asymmetry is known baseline or new.[1]

Screen

Medication effects may alter size and reactivity — but new asymmetry not explained by medication may indicate brainstem injury, increased ICP, or possible herniation.[1]

Escalate

New unilateral fixed or sluggish pupil; rapidly worsening asymmetry from baseline; pupillary change with decreased LOC, vomiting, posturing, new weakness, or an abrupt hypertension and bradycardia pattern.[1]

Prepare for imaging and bedside provider assessment as directed; ICP treatment follows diagnosis-specific protocol.[9]

Acute decrease in consciousness
Confirm

Reassess arousal and the stimulus required. A change in LOC is the most sensitive indicator of a neurologic problem.[1]

Screen

Sedation bolus, paralytic exposure, metabolic derangement, post-ictal state, transport or procedure effect, hypoxia, hypotension, dysglycemia.[1,3,5]

Escalate

New decrease in LOC or inability to arouse — especially with airway compromise, pupillary change, or motor asymmetry.[1]

New weakness, aphasia, or gaze deviation
Confirm

Recheck each extremity separately, command following, drift, and antigravity movement. Recheck language: fluent or nonfluent, comprehension, naming.[1]

Screen

Is the finding reproducible? Is it explained by sedation, a metabolic cause, or an unrecognized baseline deficit?[1]

Escalate

New focal weakness, loss of antigravity movement, loss of command following, new posturing, new aphasia, dysarthria, or gaze deviation — any of these may signal progression.[1] Stroke pathway activation criteria are local. Verify local policy

Seizure or unexplained episodic unresponsiveness
Confirm

Describe what you observed and the time. Note whether the patient has returned to baseline and how long that took.[9]

Screen

Agitation or altered responsiveness in the Neuro ICU may represent nonconvulsive seizure activity — do not assume it is behavioral.[3] Post-ictal state is a documented exam confounder.[3]

Escalate

Seizure activity or unexplained episodic unresponsiveness warrants immediate reassessment and escalation.[9] Prepare for EEG, imaging, seizure treatment, or airway management as ordered.[7,9]

Seizure treatment agents, doses, and sequence require a later batch. Verify seizure workflow and EEG or seizure alert pathway

Suspected herniation
Findings that raise concern for brainstem dysfunction or herniation

New unexplained pupillary asymmetry or a nonreactive pupil, new posturing, rapidly decreasing LOC, vomiting with pupillary change, and a Cushing reflex — hypertension with widened pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations — which is a late sign of elevated ICP and herniation physiology.[1]

Stay with the patient, protect the airway, escalate urgently through the unit ICP pathway, and prepare for imaging and provider assessment.[8,9] Osmotic therapy and other ICP treatments follow diagnosis-specific protocol and orders.[9] Verify local policy or provider-ordered parameters

Respiratory decline or loss of airway protection
Confirm

Assess cough and gag, ability to manage secretions, respiratory pattern, and oxygenation.[1]

Screen

Respiratory pattern is part of the neuro exam, not a separate system. An abnormal pattern with a declining exam raises the stakes.[1]

Escalate

Escalate immediately for depressed LOC, secretion burden, absent cough, apnea, or rapid decline. Loss of cough or gag raises aspiration and airway risk.[1]

Acute hypertension or hypotension
Confirm

Verify the reading and the ordered blood pressure goal, and check how long the patient has been outside it.[5]

Screen

Look for a cluster: rising blood pressure with increasing somnolence and new pupillary asymmetry is a pattern.[5] Consider the Cushing pattern before treating the number.[1]

Escalate

Severe hypertension or hypotension associated with neuro change.[5]

Numeric blood pressure thresholds are diagnosis-specific and provider-ordered.[4] Verify unit-specific blood pressure targets

Fever

Fever can worsen secondary brain injury and is assessed as neurologic context, not as an isolated vital sign.[1] Escalate fever associated with neuro change along with the exam findings.[5] Temperature targets and cooling plans are ordered parameters.[4]

Temperature thresholds, workup sequence, and device-based cooling management require a later batch. Verify local policy or provider-ordered parameters

Handoff guide

Shared assessments during handoff establish and validate baseline findings, so handoff is built around a live bedside exam plus a concise structured verbal report — not a list read aloud.[1]

Template

Required handoff elements

  1. Baseline neuro exam — arousal, command following, orientation, speech and language, pupils, motor asymmetry, and whether the assessment is confounded.[1]
  2. Biggest changes this shift and the exact time noted.[1]
  3. Current airway and oxygenation status, plus secretion and aspiration concerns.[1]
  4. Current blood pressure target and how well the patient stayed within goal.[5]
  5. Sedation and analgesia plan, and how sedation affects exam reliability.[4]
  6. Last important imaging and pending imaging.[8]
  7. Seizure concern, EEG status, or episodes of unexplained unresponsiveness if relevant.[9]
  8. Drains and devices and current issues if present.[8]
  9. Family concerns, code status, and next immediate action.[5]
Verbal template

The trend-based frame

“Baseline is ___; today the main change was ___ at ___; current exam is ___; patient is on ___ for sedation and pain; BP goal is ___; the major risk overnight is ___; the next expected action is ___.”

This format keeps the report trend-based and action-oriented rather than list-based.[4]

Sample

Worked example

“Baseline is awake, oriented to person and place, follows commands in all four, mild left facial droop and mild left arm drift that were present on admission, pupils equal and briskly reactive. Today the main change was increasing somnolence noted at 0230 — she went from spontaneously awake to needing voice, and the left arm drift became a clear inability to hold against gravity. I confirmed it twice, thirty minutes apart. Pupils stayed equal and reactive, glucose was normal, no sedation is running, MAP stayed inside the ordered goal. I notified the provider at 0245 and she went for a repeat CT at 0310. Current exam is opens eyes to voice, follows simple commands inconsistently, left arm no antigravity. She is on nothing for sedation and scheduled analgesia only, so the exam is not confounded. BP goal is the ordered range on the flowsheet and she has stayed in it. Major risk this shift is further decline in the left arm and level of consciousness. Next expected action is the CT read and a decision on whether to escalate imaging.”

Structured on the required elements and the verbal template above.[1,4,5]

  • Drain totals and any current device issue.[8]
