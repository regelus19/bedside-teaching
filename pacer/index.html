Failure to Capture
Spike present · No QRS follows
Critical ▼
What you see Pacer spike fires but no QRS complex follows. The myocardium is not responding to the electrical stimulus.

Immediate Steps

  • 1Increase output (mA) — Start at current setting, titrate up in small increments (e.g. 2 mA steps) while watching monitor for capture. Set final output at 2–3× the capture threshold.
  • 2Check connections — Inspect all lead-to-generator cable connections. A loose pin is the most common culprit. Reseat firmly.
  • 3Reposition patient — Left lateral decubitus (especially for RV leads). Lead position can shift with movement or during procedures.
  • 4Check lead polarity — Try reversing the positive/negative connections at the generator box if no capture achieved.
  • 5Replace generator battery — Low battery = inadequate output even at high settings. Swap to a new generator.
  • 6Check metabolic/electrolytes — Severe hyperkalemia, acidosis, or antiarrhythmics (Class IC) can raise capture threshold. Correct if possible.
  • 7Lead dislodgement? CXR stat. If threshold cannot be achieved → notify physician immediately for repositioning or emergent transcutaneous pacing backup.
⚠ Escalation Flag

If patient is pacemaker-dependent and capture is completely lost → initiate transcutaneous pacing as bridge immediately while calling for help.

Failure to Sense
Paces when it shouldn't · Competitive pacing
High ▼
What you see The pacemaker fires regardless of the patient's intrinsic rhythm. Spikes appear on top of or near native QRS complexes — risk of R-on-T phenomenon and VF.

Immediate Steps

  • 1Decrease sensitivity value (mV) — Counterintuitive: lowering the mV number makes the generator more sensitive to detecting native signals. Titrate down gradually.
  • 2Check connections / lead position — Poor lead contact reduces signal amplitude. Reseat connections firmly.
  • 3Reduce EMI sources — Check for sources of electromagnetic interference: heating pads, cautery equipment, improperly grounded devices.
  • 4Confirm intrinsic rate — If patient is truly bradycardic with no intrinsic rhythm, asynchronous pacing may be appropriate; this would not be undersensing.
  • 5If persistent and R-on-T risk is high → consider switching to asynchronous (DOO/AOO) mode as temporary measure while troubleshooting.
⚠ R-on-T Risk

Competitive pacing in the setting of underlying intrinsic rhythm is dangerous. Competitive pacing near a T-wave can trigger VF. Do not leave unresolved. Notify physician.

Failure to Pace
No spike · No output from generator
Critical ▼
What you see No pacemaker spike at all on the monitor when you expect pacing. The generator is not firing — differentiated from failure to capture, where spikes are visible.

Immediate Steps

  • 1Check ON/OFF switch — Verify the generator is powered on (sounds obvious; it happens).
  • 2Check battery — Dead battery = no output. Replace generator immediately.
  • 3Inspect all connections — Lead pins to generator ports. A fully disconnected lead will not fire. Reconnect and secure.
  • 4Oversensing? — The generator may be sensing artifact as intrinsic activity and inhibiting itself. Decrease sensitivity (increase mV number) or switch to asynchronous mode to test.
  • 5Change the generator box — Generator malfunction is uncommon but real. Swap to a backup unit.
  • 6If truly pacemaker-dependent and no output achieved → initiate transcutaneous pacing immediately + call physician.
Remember: Always confirm capture on the monitor — a wide QRS following each spike. Mechanical capture (pulse, BP) must be confirmed separately. A pacer spike with a wide QRS ≠ cardiac output.
🔴
Failure to Capture — Spike present, no QRS follows. Myocardium not responding to stimulus.
Recognition Pacemaker spikes are visible on the monitor at the programmed rate, but they are not followed by a QRS complex (for ventricular pacing) or P wave (for atrial pacing). The heart rate may fall below the paced rate or bradycardia may recur.

Step-by-Step Troubleshooting

  • 1Increase mA (output) — Turn up the output dial in 2 mA increments watching for capture. A threshold ≥20 mA suggests lead dislodgement or exit block.
  • 2Inspect connections at the generator — Pull the cable pins out and firmly reseat each. The most common cause of acute loss of capture is a loose pin connection.
  • 3Try left lateral position — Gravity can help a floating lead make better contact with the myocardium. Works best with temporary transvenous leads.
  • 4Reverse polarity — Swap positive and negative leads at the generator. Some configurations capture better in one orientation.
  • 5Replace the generator — Battery may be depleted, affecting output. Even if the battery indicator looks okay, swap to a new unit and retest.
  • 6Review medications and labs — Flecainide, amiodarone, and Class IC drugs raise capture threshold. Hyperkalemia (K+ >6.5 mEq/L) is a common metabolic cause. Correct if possible.
  • 7Obtain CXR — Confirm lead position. A coiled, retracted, or displaced lead will not capture at any output.
  • 8Notify physician — If output is maxed and capture not achieved, lead repositioning is required. Initiate transcutaneous pacing as a bridge if pacemaker-dependent.

Failure to Sense (Undersensing) — Generator does not detect intrinsic cardiac activity. Risk of competitive pacing and R-on-T.
Recognition Pacer spikes appear on or near intrinsic QRS complexes — it is pacing when it should be inhibited. Look for spikes occurring at a fixed rate despite adequate native rhythm, or fused/pseudofused beats.
⚠️
R-on-T Risk: A pacer spike landing on the T-wave (relative refractory period) can trigger ventricular fibrillation. Undersensing must be treated urgently.

Step-by-Step Troubleshooting

  • 1Decrease sensitivity value (mV) — Lower number = more sensitive. If set at 10 mV, try 5 mV. The generator needs to detect smaller intrinsic signals. This is the primary intervention.
  • 2Inspect and reseat connections — Poor electrical contact reduces the signal amplitude reaching the generator. Firmly reconnect all pins.
  • 3Check lead position (CXR) — Lead tip away from endocardium results in small electrogram signal. Lead repositioning may be needed.
  • 4Reduce EMI — Remove heating pads, check for grounding issues with other devices. EMI can falsely inhibit or trigger pacing.
  • 5Consider temporary asynchronous pacing — If R-on-T risk is high and undersensing is refractory, switching to asynchronous mode eliminates competitive pacing risk while the underlying issue is corrected.
  • 6Notify physician if undersensing is not resolved with sensitivity adjustment. May require lead repositioning.

Sensitivity: Counterintuitive Logic

Key Concept The sensitivity mV number represents the minimum signal amplitude the device will respond to. A value of 2 mV is MORE sensitive than 10 mV — it can detect smaller signals. To fix undersensing (not detecting intrinsic beats), decrease the mV number.
🔴
Failure to Pace — No pacer spikes visible. Generator is not firing at all.
Recognition No pacemaker spike artifact is visible on the ECG monitor. Differentiate from failure to capture (spikes present but no QRS) by confirming whether spikes are present at all.

Step-by-Step Troubleshooting

  • 1Confirm pacemaker is ON — Check on/off switch and display. Confirm programmed rate is set above patient's intrinsic rate.
  • 2Check and replace battery — A dead or near-dead battery is the most common mechanical cause. Replace immediately.
  • 3Inspect all connections — Disconnect and firmly reconnect lead pins at the generator. A completely unseated pin will prevent firing.
  • 4Rule out oversensing — Generator may be detecting noise/artifact as intrinsic activity and inhibiting output. Decrease sensitivity (increase mV) or switch to asynchronous mode to test.
  • 5Check monitor leads — Confirm you're watching the right lead on the monitor that would show pacer spikes (pacing artifacts can vary by lead).
  • 6Replace the generator unit — Internal circuit failure is rare but possible. Swap to a backup generator.
  • 7Pacemaker-dependent patient with no pacing output? → Initiate transcutaneous pacing immediately. Call physician, code team if unresponsive.

Oversensing vs. Failure to Pace

Differentiation If switching to asynchronous mode restores pacing, the cause was oversensing (generator was inhibiting itself). If asynchronous mode still shows no spikes, the issue is a true generator/lead/connection failure.
⚡ Asynchronous Mode
AKA: DOO (ventricular) · AOO (atrial) · "Fuck It Mode"

The pacemaker fires at a fixed rate regardless of intrinsic rhythm. No sensing. No inhibition. Pure, unconditional pacing output. This is your escalation tool when standard troubleshooting fails to restore reliable capture or when oversensing is causing failure to pace.

When to Use

  • →Persistent failure to capture despite maximum mA when you need to keep the patient alive while troubleshooting continues.
  • →Refractory undersensing with R-on-T risk — asynchronous mode eliminates competitive pacing risk.
  • →Oversensing causing inhibition — the generator keeps inhibiting itself. DOO bypasses sensing entirely.
  • →MRI, cautery, or EMI exposure that is interfering with sensing function.
  • →Diagnostic confirmation — if switching to async restores pacing, oversensing was the cause of failure to pace.
⚠️
R-on-T Risk in Async Mode: If the patient has any intrinsic rhythm, asynchronous pacing can deliver a spike on the T-wave and precipitate VF. Use when patient is pacemaker-dependent (minimal or no intrinsic activity), or when the alternative (no pacing) is worse.

How to Switch to Async Mode

  • 1Locate the Mode selector on the generator box.
  • 2Change from VVI / DDD to VOO / DOO (ventricular async) or AOO (atrial async).
  • 3Confirm the rate is set appropriately (typically 60–80 bpm or per physician order).
  • 4Observe monitor for consistent spike output at the programmed rate.
  • 5Watch for capture (QRS following each spike). Adjust mA as needed.
  • 6Notify physician. Asynchronous mode is a bridge — do not leave patient in DOO/VOO without a plan.
The Escalation Ladder VVI pacing not capturing → Increase mA → Adjust connections → Switch to DOO (Asynchronous) → Still no capture → Lead dislodged → Transcutaneous pacing + Call physician for emergent lead repositioning
⚠ If Async Mode Also Fails

If DOO/VOO mode still shows no capture despite maximum output → lead is most likely dislodged or broken. This is a lead problem, not a generator problem. Initiate transcutaneous pacing immediately and mobilize for emergent lead repositioning.

🫀
Epicardial temporary pacemaker wires are routinely placed during cardiac surgery. Understanding why they're placed — and when to use them — is as important as troubleshooting them.
Routine Surgical Backup
Standard of care post-cardiac surgery
Prophylactic ▼
Why they're placed Epicardial pacing wires are placed on the right atrium and/or right ventricle as standard practice at the end of most open cardiac surgeries — even when no arrhythmia is present. The heart has been arrested, cooled, manipulated, and reperfused. Postoperative conduction disturbances are common and can emerge hours after chest closure.

Common Wire Configurations

ConfigWiresPacing Capability
Ventricular only2 RV wiresVVI / VOO — ventricular backup pacing
Atrial only2 RA wiresAAI / AOO — atrial pacing, rate support with intact AV conduction
Dual chamber2 RA + 2 RV wiresDDD / DOO — full AV synchrony capability

Why Backup Matters

  • →Cardioplegia effect — Potassium-based cardioplegia arrests the heart. Residual conduction suppression can persist into the early postop period, causing sinus bradycardia or AV nodal slowing.
  • →Edema and inflammation near conduction tissue from surgical manipulation, particularly near the AV node in valve repairs, septal procedures, or CABG with extensive dissection.
  • →Reperfusion arrhythmias — As the heart rewarms and reperfuses, ectopy, junctional rhythms, and transient heart block are common in the first 12–24 hours.
  • →Drug effects — Beta-blockers, amiodarone, and other antiarrhythmics used perioperatively can compound conduction slowing postoperatively.
Typical Backup Settings (Inactive / Standby) Rate: 40–50 bpm (below intrinsic rate — pacer fires only if rate drops below this threshold) · Output: 10–20 mA · Sensitivity: 2–5 mV · Mode: VVI or DDD per physician order
Bradycardia & Heart Block
Most common active use case post-cardiac surgery
Active ▼
Clinical Picture Symptomatic bradycardia, junctional rhythm, 2nd-degree (Mobitz I or II), or complete (3rd-degree) heart block causing hemodynamic compromise — hypotension, low CO, altered mental status, or worsening lactate.

High-Risk Procedures for Conduction Injury

  • →Aortic valve replacement/repair — AV node and bundle of His are in close proximity to the aortic annulus. Sutures, debridement, or annular calcification manipulation can injure conduction tissue.
  • →Mitral valve surgery — Posterolateral annulus work near the coronary sinus and AV node.
  • →Septal myectomy / TAVI — Direct risk to the bundle of His and left bundle branch.
  • →Congenital repairs — VSD closure and Fontan procedures carry significant conduction injury risk.

Management Approach

  • 1Confirm hemodynamically significant bradycardia — rate, BP, MAP, CO/CI if PA cath in place.
  • 2Activate epicardial pacing — increase rate 10–20 bpm above intrinsic rate. Mode: VVI for ventricular block, DDD for complete heart block to maintain AV synchrony.
  • 3Verify capture and confirm hemodynamic improvement — BP, MAP, waveform quality on arterial line.
  • 4For complete heart block: DDD pacing with appropriate AV delay (150–200 ms) to preserve atrial contribution to CO.
  • 5Monitor for resolution over 48–72 hours. Persistent complete heart block beyond 5–7 days → evaluation for permanent pacemaker implantation.
Optimizing Hemodynamics
AV synchrony · Rate optimization · Low CO states
Active ▼
Core Concept The temporary pacemaker is not just a safety net — it is an active hemodynamic tool. In the post-cardiac surgery patient, optimizing rate and AV synchrony can meaningfully improve cardiac output without adding a single vasopressor.

AV Synchrony — Why It Matters

  • →Atrial kick contributes 20–30% of ventricular filling in a normal heart — and significantly more in a stiff, hypertrophied, or post-bypass ventricle. Losing AV synchrony (junctional rhythm, complete heart block, RV-only pacing) can substantially reduce CO.
  • →Pacemaker syndrome — VVI pacing without atrial contribution can cause retrograde VA conduction, cannon A waves, hypotension, and reduced CO. Switch to DDD if wires allow.
  • →AV delay optimization — Too short (<100 ms): atria contracting against closed valves. Too long (>250 ms): diastolic MR, lost atrial contribution. Target 150–200 ms; titrate to best arterial waveform and BP.

Rate Optimization Post-Bypass

  • →Target HR post-cardiac surgery: 80–100 bpm. A faster rate reduces diastolic filling time but also reduces wall tension and oxygen demand in a stunned ventricle. The Starling curve shifts — many post-bypass hearts perform better at higher rates.
  • →Bradycardia is poorly tolerated early post-bypass — CO = HR × SV. In a stiff post-bypass heart with limited SV reserve, a drop in HR directly drops CO. Use pacing to maintain rate 80–90 bpm.
  • →Atrial pacing (AAI) preferred when AV conduction is intact — maintains native AV conduction and ventricular activation sequence, preserving more physiologic LV function than RV pacing.
Clinical Tip In a low-CO state post-bypass where the patient is in junctional rhythm at 50 bpm with intact RA and RV wires: switch to DDD at 80–90 bpm with AV delay 180 ms. Watch the arterial line waveform in real time. You may see an immediate improvement in pulse pressure and MAP — before you've opened a single drip.
Overdrive Pacing
Tachyarrhythmia termination · Atrial flutter
Active ▼
What It Is Rapid atrial pacing at a rate faster than the tachyarrhythmia to capture and then abruptly terminate it — exploiting the refractory period of the re-entrant circuit. Most commonly used for atrial flutter and certain SVTs post-cardiac surgery.

Indications Post-Cardiac Surgery

  • →Atrial flutter — Extremely common post-cardiac surgery (up to 30% of patients). Typical flutter rate ~300 bpm with 2:1 block → ventricular rate ~150. Overdrive pacing via RA wires can terminate it.
  • →Reentrant SVT — When pharmacologic rate control is insufficient or cardioversion is being deferred.
  • →Post-ablation arrhythmia storms — Occasional use in electrophysiology-adjacent scenarios.

Overdrive Pacing Protocol (Atrial Flutter)

  • 1Confirm atrial flutter on 12-lead or rhythm strip — sawtooth flutter waves, regular atrial rate ~300 bpm.
  • 2Requires functional RA wires — confirm capture at current mA before attempting overdrive.
  • 3Set atrial pacing rate to 10–20 bpm above flutter rate (typically 320–400 bpm). Physician-directed and institution-protocol-dependent.
  • 4Pace for 15–30 seconds to capture the atrial circuit, then abruptly drop rate to 80–90 bpm. The hope is the circuit terminates into sinus rhythm.
  • 5Monitor closely — may also convert to atrial fibrillation rather than sinus. Defibrillator must be immediately available.
  • !Physician must be at bedside for overdrive pacing. This is not a solo nurse intervention. Crash cart within arm's reach.
⚠ Contraindications

Do NOT attempt overdrive pacing for atrial fibrillation (no organized circuit to capture), VT, or VF. Also contraindicated if RA wires are not reliably capturing.

Code / Arrest Situation
Asystole · PEA · Post-cardiac surgery arrest
Critical ▼
Unique Context Post-cardiac surgery arrest is fundamentally different from a standard in-hospital code. The chest has been open, bypass has been run, and the temporary pacing wires are already in place. The temporary pacemaker is often a first-line intervention — not an afterthought.

Asystole / Pulseless Bradycardia

  • 1Immediately attempt pacing — Switch to asynchronous mode (DOO/VOO), max output (20–25 mA), rate 80–100 bpm. Do not wait for compressions if pacing wires are functional.
  • 2Verify capture on monitor — look for wide QRS following each spike.
  • 3Confirm mechanical capture — palpate pulse, check arterial line waveform. Electrical capture ≠ mechanical capture.
  • 4If no capture → begin CPR per ACLS. In post-cardiac surgery arrest <10 days post-op: consider emergent resternotomy per institutional protocol.

PEA with Functioning Pacemaker

  • →If the pacemaker is capturing (spikes + QRS present) but there is no pulse → pacing is not the problem. Search for and treat reversible causes: tamponade, tension pneumothorax, hypovolemia, graft occlusion, massive vasodilation.
  • →Tamponade is the most critical reversible cause in post-cardiac surgery PEA. Classic Beck's triad (hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds) may be absent in the intubated ICU patient. Bedside echo or emergent resternotomy if tamponade is suspected and patient is deteriorating.

VF / Shockable Rhythm

  • 1Disconnect pacing generator before defibrillation — Current can arc through pacing wires and damage the myocardium at the lead tip, cause lead dislodgement, or damage the generator.
  • 2Defibrillate per ACLS protocol.
  • 3Reconnect and reassess pacing post-shock. Defibrillation may reset the pacing threshold — recheck capture after reconnection.
⚠ Post-Cardiac Surgery Arrest Protocol

Many institutions have a separate cardiac surgery arrest algorithm. External chest compressions may be delayed or avoided in the first 48–72 hours post-sternotomy (risk of sternal disruption, graft injury). Know your institution's protocol before it happens.

Junctional Rhythm
AV dissociation · Post-bypass AV nodal suppression
Common ▼
Why It's So Common Post-Bypass The AV node is highly sensitive to ischemia, cold, potassium-based cardioplegia, and direct surgical manipulation. Junctional rhythm (narrow complex, rate 40–60, retrograde or absent P waves) is one of the most common immediate post-bypass rhythms and often requires temporary pacing support.

Management

  • →If hemodynamically stable and rate adequate (50–60 bpm): observe. Junctional rhythm typically resolves within hours as cardioplegia clears and AV nodal function recovers. Avoid unnecessary pacing.
  • →If hemodynamically compromised (MAP <65, low CO, hypotension): activate atrial pacing (AAI) to restore AV synchrony and increase rate. If AV conduction is intact, atrial pacing at 80–90 bpm will drive the ventricle normally.
  • →If AV conduction is not intact: use DDD pacing to pace both chambers with appropriate AV delay.
  • →Avoid aggressive atropine in post-bypass patients — response is variable and tachycardia can worsen ischemia in fresh grafts.
Wire Removal
Timing · Risks · When to keep them longer
Procedural ▼
General Timing Epicardial pacing wires are typically removed 3–5 days post-operatively once the patient is pacing-independent and hemodynamically stable, anticoagulation is at a safe level, and no arrhythmias are anticipated.

Criteria Before Removal

  • ✓Patient not pacemaker-dependent — intrinsic rate adequate and stable for ≥24 hours.
  • ✓No active arrhythmias requiring pacing (complete heart block, recurrent bradycardia).
  • ✓Anticoagulation within safe parameters per institutional protocol — typically INR <1.5 or heparin held.
  • ✓No active need for overdrive pacing capability.

Risks of Removal

  • →Cardiac tamponade — Most feared complication. Wire removal can tear an epicardial vessel or myocardium. Have emergency resternotomy capability available if removing wires early post-op.
  • →Bleeding — Especially with anticoagulation. Supratherapeutic INR or therapeutic heparin significantly increases hemorrhage risk.
  • →Wire breakage — Retained wire fragments are rarely clinically significant but should be documented. Inform the team.
  • →Arrhythmia — Rare; wire removal can occasionally trigger transient ectopy.
⚠ Post-Removal Monitoring

Monitor for 30–60 minutes post-wire removal for signs of tamponade: rising HR, falling BP, narrowing pulse pressure, JVD, equalization of filling pressures. Have echo capability immediately available.

CVICU Context: In a cardiovascular ICU, the temporary pacemaker is one of the most versatile tools at the bedside. Knowing not just how to troubleshoot it but when and why to use it — and when to escalate from passive backup to active hemodynamic intervention — is what separates competent from expert post-cardiac surgery nursing care.
