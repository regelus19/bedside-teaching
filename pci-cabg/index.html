SECTION 00

What this tool teaches

Revascularization is not a single yes/no. It is a sequence of gates. At each gate the team asks one clean question, and the answer either advances the decision, redirects it, or sends it back for more data. Learn the gates and the whole conversation stops being a mystery.

The mental model in one breath

First decide whether to revascularize at all (is there ischemia, are there symptoms, is there prognostic benefit). Only then decide how — stent or surgery — by weighing anatomy against the patient. Throughout, urgency overrides everything: an unstable patient is no longer a planning problem, it's a clock.

Why this matters

Nurses and supervisors don't pick the procedure — but you stage the timeline. Knowing the gates tells you when a case is electively planned, when antiplatelets must wash out, when a "consult" is actually a Heart Team meeting, and when "we're taking them now" is the only right answer.

The three forces in tension

Catheter axis · PCIFast, less invasive, repeatable, no sternotomy. Rewards focal, simple anatomy and frail/high-surgical-risk patients.
Surgical axis · CABGDurable, complete revascularization, prognostic benefit in the right patient. Rewards complex, multivessel, diabetic, low-EF disease.
Medical axis · OMTAlways the floor under both. Guideline-directed therapy treats every patient; sometimes it's the whole answer.
Think

PCI and CABG are not rivals fighting for the same patient. They are tools matched to anatomy and physiology. The art is the match — and the Heart Team exists precisely for the cases where the match isn't obvious.

The six places a patient can land

Every path through the gates ends in one of these output categories:

PCI FAVOREDSimple/durable anatomy, or surgery too risky.
CABG FAVOREDComplex/multivessel, diabetes, low EF, left main with high complexity.
HEART TEAMGenuine equipoise; competing factors; needs the table.
MEDICAL THERAPYNo ischemia/symptoms/prognostic indication to revascularize.
URGENT / EMERGENTSTEMI, shock, mechanical complication — the clock wins.
NEED MORE DATAMissing physiology, anatomy detail, or risk assessment.

REFERENCE

Key concepts behind the gates

The tests, scores, and trials the team keeps invoking — explained from first principles, with the numbers that matter and where the real validated tools live.

G2

FFR & iFR — measuring whether a narrowing actually starves the muscle

An angiogram shows the shape of a narrowing, not whether it limits flow. FFR (Fractional Flow Reserve) and iFR (instantaneous wave-free Ratio) answer the functional question — is this lesion causing ischemia?

What FFR is

FFR is the ratio of blood flow the artery can deliver past the narrowing versus what it would deliver if it were normal. In practice it's measured as the pressure beyond the lesion (Pd) divided by the pressure in the aorta (Pa) at maximal hyperemia — when the vessel is forced fully open. A normal vessel = 1.0.

FFR ≤ 0.80 = significant (treat)  ·  > 0.80 = defer

How it's done

During the cath, a pressure-sensor guidewire is advanced across the lesion. Maximal hyperemia is induced — usually IV or intracoronary adenosine (or regadenoson / papaverine). The wire records Pd, the catheter records Pa, and the system computes the ratio at peak vasodilation.

What iFR adds

iFR measures the same pressure ratio but only during the diastolic "wave-free period," when resting resistance is naturally low and stable — so no adenosine is needed. That makes it faster and avoids adenosine's flushing, breathlessness, and transient heart block. DEFINE-FLAIR and iFR-SWEDEHEART showed it's non-inferior to FFR.

iFR ≤ 0.89 = significant  ·  > 0.89 = defer
Guardrail · the gray zone

These cutoffs are not bright lines. Borderline values (e.g., FFR ~0.78–0.82, iFR ~0.87–0.91) sit in a gray zone — the interventionalist weighs the lesion, the vessel's territory, symptoms, and trial context, not the number alone. Don't treat a single reading as a verdict.

Pearl · the pullback

Slowly withdrawing the wire records pressure along the whole vessel. An abrupt step-up at one spot = a focal, stentable lesion. A gradual gradient = diffuse disease smeared down the artery — a poor stent target and a nudge toward surgery or medicine.

Say it to a family

"We threaded a tiny pressure sensor past the narrowing. It tells us whether the blockage is truly choking off blood flow — or just looks narrow on the picture."

G3

SYNTAX score — grading anatomical complexity

The SYNTAX score (named for the SYNTAX trial — SYNergy between PCI with TAXUS and Cardiac Surgery) is an angiographic tally of how complex the coronary disease is. Every lesion in a vessel ≥1.5 mm with ≥50% narrowing is scored and weighted.

What drives the score up

Lesion location (proximal/dominant segments score higher), plus high-risk features: total occlusions (CTOs), bifurcations / trifurcations, aorto-ostial lesions, severe tortuosity, length > 20 mm, heavy calcification, thrombus, and diffuse disease / small vessels. More disease in worse places = higher score.

The bands

Low 0–22 → PCI reasonable
Intermediate 23–32 → Heart Team
High ≥33 → CABG favored

Higher complexity predicts both a tougher, less durable PCI and a larger benefit from the completeness of bypass.

SYNTAX II — adding the patient

Plain SYNTAX is anatomy only and is operator-variable. SYNTAX score II blends in clinical variables — age, sex, creatinine clearance, LVEF, COPD, peripheral vascular disease, unprotected left main — to estimate individualized mortality and give a more honest PCI-vs-CABG steer. When you hear "SYNTAX II," that's anatomy plus the person.

Pitfall

Treating SYNTAX as a precise verdict. It's an anchor for the conversation, not a calculator output — and anatomic SYNTAX without clinical context is only half the picture.

G5/7

STS score — predicting surgical risk

The STS Adult Cardiac Surgery Risk Score (Society of Thoracic Surgeons) estimates a specific patient's risk from cardiac surgery. Its headline output is PROM — Predicted Risk of Mortality — plus risks of stroke, renal failure, prolonged ventilation, reoperation, deep sternal wound infection, and prolonged stay.

What goes into it

Age, sex, body size, the planned procedure and its urgency (elective → urgent → emergent → salvage), prior cardiac surgery, plus comorbidities: diabetes, renal function / dialysis, lung disease, peripheral and cerebrovascular disease, prior MI, heart failure / NYHA class, and EF. It's built and regularly recalibrated on the enormous STS National Database.

What "high risk" means

Borrowing the bands widely used in valve decisions:

Low < 4%
Intermediate 4–8%
High > 8% (often prohibitive > 10–15%)

A "high" PROM doesn't forbid surgery — it raises the bar, shifts weight toward PCI or transcatheter options, and makes the goals-of-care conversation mandatory. It always travels with a frailty assessment, because two patients with the same PROM can have very different real-world reserve.

Can we make it automatic in this tool?

Honestly — no, and we shouldn't fake it. The real STS model is a proprietary, periodically re-fit regression on a national registry; any home-grown reimplementation would be unvalidated and potentially misleading. The right pattern for an app is to collect the inputs and hand off to the official calculator, then store the returned PROM. That keeps the number trustworthy.

→ Official STS Risk Calculator · riskcalc.sts.org

When a "third opinion" is usually sought

Beyond the standard interventional + surgical Heart Team, additional review is typically pulled in when: the patient is high or prohibitive surgical risk with complex anatomy; there's genuine equipoise after the first discussion; advanced heart failure is in play (transplant / durable MCS evaluation); or the case may do better at a higher-volume complex-revascularization or structural-heart center. The "third opinion" is usually a second surgeon, a heart-failure/transplant cardiologist, or referral to a center with more of the specific expertise the case needs.

Pearl · the cousins

EuroSCORE II is the European counterpart to STS — same job (predict surgical mortality), different population and equation. GRACE and TIMI are different animals entirely: they risk-stratify ACS (how urgently to go invasive), not surgical survivability.

G6

FREEDOM — why diabetes + multivessel leans CABG

FREEDOM = Future REvascularization Evaluation in patients with Diabetes mellitus: Optimal management of Multivessel disease (NEJM, 2012). It's the trial behind one of the strongest CABG signals on the board.

What it did

It randomized patients with diabetes and multivessel coronary disease to PCI with drug-eluting stents versus CABG, all on good medical therapy.

What it found

CABG won: a significantly lower rate of the composite of death, MI, and stroke, driven by fewer deaths and MIs — durable on long-term follow-up. The trade-off was a higher early stroke rate with surgery. The bottom line that stuck: for diabetics with multivessel disease, bypass generally beats stents for hard outcomes.

Diabetes + multivessel disease  →  CABG favored (with acceptable surgical risk)
Say it to a family

"For people with diabetes who have several blocked arteries, large studies show bypass surgery tends to help them live longer and avoid heart attacks better than stents — so we lean that way when surgery is safe for them."

DECODING THE REPORT

Reading the cath report

The cath report is a map of the coronary arteries — which are narrowed, by how much, how well blood is flowing, and what was done about it. Here's the vocabulary that makes it readable at handoff.

FLOW

TIMI flow grade — how freely blood is moving

The TIMI flow grade (from the Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction trials) is a simple 0–3 score the angiographer assigns by watching contrast move down the vessel. It's the number you'll hear most: "we restored TIMI 3 flow."

TIMI 0
No perfusion. No flow past the blockage at all.
TIMI 1
Penetration without perfusion. Contrast trickles past but never fills the far bed.
TIMI 2
Partial perfusion. The far bed does fill — but slowly (delayed).
TIMI 3
Complete, normal perfusion. The goal of any reperfusion.
Reperfusion goal = TIMI 3. Anything less means flow is still compromised.

Two finer cousins you'll see

TIMI frame count (cTFC) — instead of an eyeball 0–3, it counts how many cine frames contrast takes to reach a fixed distal landmark. Lower = faster = better; it's the more objective version of the same idea.

Myocardial blush grade / TIMI myocardial perfusion grade (0–3) — looks at tissue-level perfusion, not just the big vessel. You can have a wide-open artery (TIMI 3) but poor blush — that's microvascular "no-reflow," and it matters because the muscle still isn't getting fed.

Say it to a family

"TIMI flow is just a 0-to-3 grade for how freely blood moves through the artery. Zero means blocked; three means normal. After the stent, we restored it to three."

MAP

The vessels, dominance & how "tight" is tight

Coronary vessels & abbreviations

LM / LMCALeft main — the trunk that feeds the LAD and LCx. Disease here threatens a huge territory.
LADLeft anterior descending — front wall + septum. The proximal LAD is the classic "widow-maker." Branches: diagonals (D1, D2), septals.
LCxLeft circumflex — lateral wall. Branches: obtuse marginals (OM1, OM2).
RCARight coronary — inferior wall; usually feeds the SA & AV nodes (why inferior MIs cause bradycardia/block).
PDAPosterior descending artery — the branch that defines "dominance."
RamusRamus intermedius — an extra branch off the left main in some people.

Dominance — who supplies the PDA

Right (~85%)PDA comes off the RCA. The common pattern.
Left (~7%)PDA comes off the LCx — the left system feeds more muscle, so left-sided disease carries more weight.
Co-dominant (~8%)Shared between RCA and LCx.

How tight is the narrowing?

< 50%Non-obstructive / mild — usually medical management.
50–69%Moderate — the gray zone; often needs FFR/iFR to settle (see Key Concepts).
70–99%Severe / significant — flow-limiting. (Left main counts as significant at ≥50%.)
100%Total occlusion. If chronic (>3 months) it's a CTO.

Other grades you'll spot

TIMI thrombus grade (0–5) — how much clot is present (5 = total occlusive thrombus). Rentrop collateral grade (0–3) — how well other vessels are back-feeding a blocked one (3 = complete collateral filling), which is why some CTOs are surprisingly well tolerated.

Don't confuse these two

TIMI flow grade (0–3) = how blood moves on the angiogram. TIMI risk score = a totally separate points score that predicts outcomes in ACS (a cousin of GRACE). Same name, different jobs — flow grade is on the cath report; the risk score is a bedside calculation.
