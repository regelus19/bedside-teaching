02 · Diagnostic Challenge
Why This Was Easy to Miss
This is not textbook HOCM. Every feature of this patient made it easier to attribute his hypotension to something else entirely.

⚠️ The Clinical Trap

Post-op hypotension after CABG has a long differential: bleeding, tamponade, cardiogenic shock, vasoplegia, arrhythmia. The standard ICU reflex for a hypotensive post-CABG patient with low-output physiology is to add a vasopressor — often norepinephrine — or reach for an inotrope if the EF looks weak. In this patient, the EF was not weak. It was hyperdynamic (>65%). The inotrope reflex would have been catastrophic.

🎭 What Made This Look Like Ordinary Shock

  • Hypotension after major cardiac surgery
  • Significant blood loss and drain output
  • Anemia (Hgb 7.1–8.4)
  • Bilateral pulmonary congestion on CXR
  • Patient had prior NSTEMI
  • Pre-op echo: EF 55–60%, only trace MR
  • No prior HOCM or known outflow obstruction
  • Off-pump case — no bypass to blame

🔍 What Made This SAM — Not HOCM, Not Septic Shock

  • MR was dynamic — severe only with hypovolemia
  • MR resolved immediately with fluid + Neo
  • No fixed LVOT gradient at rest
  • Hyperdynamic EF (>65%) post-revascularization
  • Structural substrate: LVH, MAC, calcified P2
  • Timing: developed "toward the end of the case"
  • Off-pump CABG → major preload swings
  • TEE showed reversibility in real time

🧠 The Pattern That Should Trigger SAM on Your Differential

Think SAM when a post-CABG patient has:

  • Preserved or hyperdynamic EF despite hypotension
  • Worsening hemodynamics with inotropes
  • Dynamic or load-dependent MR murmur
  • Hypotension worse with hypovolemia
  • Concentric LVH on echo pre-op
  • Mitral annular calcification
  • Heavy posterior leaflet calcification (especially P2)
  • Thickened anterior mitral leaflet

04 · Structural vs Dynamic
Structural Substrate vs Dynamic Trigger
SAM doesn't happen randomly. It requires a vulnerable anatomy AND a hemodynamic trigger. This patient had both.
🧱
Built Over Years

Structural Substrate

The anatomy was already primed for SAM before the first incision. Years of HTN + AS created a ventricle that was stiff, thick-walled, and small-cavitied.

  • Concentric LVH — thick walls narrow the LVOT geometry
  • Mild AS (chronic pressure loading) — further LV remodeling
  • Mitral annular calcification — restricts leaflet mobility
  • Heavily calcified posterior leaflet (P2) — shifts coaptation anteriorly, displaces AML toward LVOT
  • Thickened anterior mitral leaflet — larger surface area for Venturi drag
  • Biatrial enlargement — chronic diastolic dysfunction, stiff filling
  • Likely sigmoid septum — age-related basal septal convexity narrows LVOT further
⚡
Happened Intraoperatively

Dynamic Trigger

The off-pump CABG created the perfect storm: preload dropped, LV became hyperdynamic after revascularization, and the outflow tract narrowed into obstruction.

  • Off-pump manipulation — heart repositioned repeatedly, venous return fluctuates
  • Blood loss + third spacing — significant drain output, anemia (Hgb 7.1–8.4)
  • Hypovolemia — LV cavity shrinks → LVOT narrows further
  • Post-revascularization EF jump — 55–60% → >65%, hyperdynamic
  • Catecholamine surge — endogenous stress response increases contractility
  • Low afterload — vasodilation + vasoplegia → ventricle empties too fast
  • Timing: "toward the end of the case" — cumulative effect of all triggers

🫀 What Is SAM, Mechanically?

During systole, blood accelerates rapidly through a narrowed LVOT. The high-velocity flow creates a Venturi (drag) effect — like a flag in the wind — that pulls the anterior mitral leaflet anteriorly toward the septum.

When the leaflet contacts the septum, it obstructs forward flow and disrupts coaptation, creating a jet of severe MR into the left atrium. This is not structural MR — it is functional, load-dependent, and reversible.

💉 Why Hypotension Makes It Worse

Hypotension → less venous return → LV fills less → smaller LV cavity → LVOT narrows further → more Venturi force → worse SAM → less forward output → more hypotension.

The body's catecholamine response to hypotension accelerates the cycle by increasing contractility further.

✅ Why Volume + Neo Breaks the Cycle

  • Volume → fills LV → enlarges cavity → LVOT widens → less obstruction
  • Phenylephrine → increases SVR/afterload → LV empties more slowly → more filling time → cavity size improves
  • Both → reduce LVOT velocity → Venturi force drops → AML moves back → SAM resolves → MR disappears

✅ Phenylephrine: Why It Works

Pure α₁ agonist — raises SVR and MAP with zero beta activity. Increases afterload, which slows LV emptying, enlarges the LV cavity, reduces LVOT velocity, and diminishes Venturi drag on the anterior leaflet. No risk of worsening hyperdynamic physiology.

⚠️ Norepinephrine: The Nuance

Strong alpha raises BP well — but moderate β₁ activity adds contractility. In SAM, this may worsen LVOT obstruction underneath even while MAP appears to improve. Watch for persistent labile hemodynamics, worsening MR, or pulmonary edema on levo — this is the "looks ok but isn't" pattern. Neo is cleaner.

🚫 Inotropes: Potentially Catastrophic

The LV is already hyperdynamic. Dobutamine, milrinone, or epinephrine increase contractility in a ventricle that's squeezing too hard, in a cavity that's too small. LVOT velocity skyrockets. SAM worsens. MR worsens. BP drops further. This is the wrong-pathway death spiral.

📌 The Rule to Remember

In SAM/LVOTO, the problem is NOT weak squeeze. The problem is an underfilled, hyperdynamic LV ejecting too fast through a narrowed outflow path. Inotropes treat weak squeeze. This patient does not have weak squeeze. Giving inotropes here is treating the wrong diagnosis with the wrong drug and making both worse simultaneously.

07 · Aggravating Factors
What Would Make It Worse
Know what to avoid just as much as what to give. In SAM, several routine ICU interventions are actively harmful.
💉
Inotropes (Dobutamine / Milrinone / Epi)

Increases contractility in an already-hyperdynamic LV. Faster LVOT flow velocity → more Venturi drag → worse SAM → worse MR → worse output. Do not confuse hyperdynamic-obstruction shock with cardiogenic shock.

💧
Aggressive Diuresis Early Post-Op

Reduces preload → LV underfills → cavity shrinks → LVOT narrows → SAM worsens. This patient had clinical edema and pulmonary congestion but "caution with SAM" was explicitly documented. Tolerate some congestion early.

💓
Tachycardia

Reduces diastolic filling time → LV fills less → smaller cavity → worse SAM. Avoid anything that accelerates HR: pain, agitation, beta-agonists, exogenous catecholamines.

📉
Hypotension — Any Cause

Any drop in MAP feeds the cycle. Low preload → small LV → more SAM → less output → more hypotension. Treat aggressively and early. Do not allow permissive hypotension in this patient.

🩸
Hypovolemia From Any Source

Bleeding, chest tube output, third spacing, inadequate volume replacement. All reduce LV preload. Keep euvolemic. Albumin or IVF PRN as ordered. Monitor drains and I&O closely.

💨
Vasodilators / Low SVR States

Anything that drops SVR allows the LV to empty more aggressively. Avoid nitroglycerin, nitroprusside, or milrinone. Beta₂ agonists also reduce afterload — even nebulized albuterol can matter in a borderline case.

08 · Management
Management Algorithm
This is the framework used for this patient. Pathway depends on hemodynamic context.

⚠️ Wrong Pathway — "Standard Shock Reflex"

1
Recognize hypotension — SBP <90, MAP <65 post-CABG
2
Add norepinephrine / increase vasopressor — MAP drops → reach for levo, which has β₁ activity → contractility ↑
3
See persistent hypotension → assume cardiogenic — EF looks hyperdynamic but MAP still low → "heart must be weak"
4
Add inotrope (dobutamine or milrinone) — increases contractility, drops SVR → LVOT velocity spikes → SAM dramatically worsens
5
Hemodynamic collapse — severe MR + LVOTO + low CO → pulmonary edema, escalating pressors, possible arrest
Outcome: Treating the wrong mechanism → death spiral. The inotrope is gasoline on the fire.

✅ Correct Pathway — SAM/LVOTO Management

1
Recognize the SAM pattern — hyperdynamic EF + dynamic MR + load-dependent hypotension → suspect LVOTO. Anesthesia TEE documented it here.
2
Volume resuscitation — IVF/albumin bolus to increase LV preload → cavity enlarges → LVOT geometry improves → Venturi force drops
3
Phenylephrine (α₁ pure) — increases SVR/afterload → slows LV emptying → cavity stays larger → LVOT velocity drops → SAM resolves
4
Wean norepinephrine — β₁ inotropy not needed and potentially harmful. Replace with phenylephrine titrated to MAP ≥ 65.
5
Strictly no inotropes — dobutamine, milrinone, epinephrine all contraindicated while SAM is the dominant mechanism
6
Cautious diuresis only after SAM stability — tolerate some pulmonary congestion early; overly aggressive diuresis reactivates SAM
7
Control heart rate — target HR 60–70s. Avoid tachycardia. Consider β-blockade once hemodynamically safe (already on ASA/statin; BB to be added per CVS).
8
Repeat echo if: persistent hypotension, new murmur, worsening pulmonary edema, escalating pressor requirement, or clinical concern for residual LVOT gradient
Outcome: SAM resolves. MR becomes trace. Hemodynamics stabilize. Weaning phenylephrine over subsequent days.
09 · Bedside Practice
Nursing & House Supervisor Watch-Outs
This is what to look for, what to escalate, and what NOT to do at the bedside for any post-CABG patient with a SAM flag.

Monitor Closely For

📊
BP Trending Down — Escalate Early

Any sustained MAP <65 or SBP <100 → titrate phenylephrine, bolus fluids or albumin per orders, and notify provider. Do not wait for MAP <55 in this patient.

🩸
Chest Tube Output

High drain output = volume loss = preload drop = SAM risk. Document hourly output, notify CVS per protocol (>200 mL/hr). Replace losses per orders.

❤️
Heart Rate Changes

Tachycardia reduces LV filling time and worsens SAM. New-onset SVT or AF → notify provider immediately. Target HR 60–70s. Treat pain and agitation to prevent catecholamine-driven HR elevation.

🌬️
Respiratory Status / SpO₂

Worsening pulmonary edema may indicate SAM-related MR is worsening (blood pooling in LA). But aggressive diuresis to "fix" it can backfire by reducing preload. Notify provider before escalating diuretics.

Do NOT Do Without MD/APRN Order

🚫
Do NOT give inotropes if ordered without SAM confirmation

If dobutamine or milrinone appears as a new PRN or order, confirm with the provider that SAM has resolved or is not the dominant mechanism. This patient has an explicit "No Inotropes" order while SAM is active.

🚫
Do NOT aggressively diurese without guidance

Pulmonary congestion is expected in the early post-op period and may be tolerated temporarily. Pushing furosemide without communicating the SAM context can re-trigger hemodynamic instability.

⚠️
Report New Murmur Promptly

If you auscultate a new or changing systolic murmur, particularly one that's louder with standing or with reduced filling, this may signal active SAM. Notify provider. Echo may need to be repeated urgently.

⚠️
Hemodynamic Instability After Positional Changes

Sitting up, dangling, early mobility — any position change that reduces preload can re-trigger SAM. Use caution and monitor closely. Return to supine if BP drops significantly with position change.

10 · Expected Trajectory
Repeat Echo / Expected Clinical Course
SAM after CABG is often transient if managed correctly. Know when to repeat the echo and what to expect.

🔁 When to Repeat Echo

  • Persistent hypotension despite phenylephrine and adequate volume
  • New or worsening pulmonary edema not explained by fluid overload alone
  • New or louder systolic murmur at the bedside
  • Escalating vasopressor requirement without clear cause
  • Concern for hemodynamic deterioration or clinical decline
  • Prior to switching from phenylephrine to another agent
  • When considering early diuresis — confirm SAM has resolved first

📈 Expected Course (If Managed Correctly)

POD1
Hemodynamic Stabilization

On phenylephrine + adequate volume, SAM resolves. MR should be trace at rest. MAP maintained 65–80s. Sedation weaned. Working toward extubation.

POD2
Pressor Wean Begins

As patient auto-diureses and volume distributes, phenylephrine requirements typically fall. Monitor for recurrence with any acute preload drop.

POD3+
Beta Blockade Introduction

Once hemodynamically stable, beta blocker added per CVS plan for rate control and remodeling benefit. Controlled contractility helps prevent SAM recurrence.

Echo
Outpatient Echo Consideration

Formal echo at follow-up to reassess LVOT, MR, and LV function once EF normalizes from hyperdynamic state. MAC/AML anatomy is permanent — long-term awareness needed.

⚠️ Structural Anatomy Does Not Go Away

The concentric LVH, mitral annular calcification, thickened anterior leaflet, and calcified P2 are permanent. The SAM risk resolves when the hemodynamic triggers (hypovolemia, hyperdynamic state, low afterload) are corrected. This patient will always have a SAM-prone anatomy. Any future hospitalization with significant hypovolemia, tachycardia, or vasodilation should prompt awareness of this structural substrate.

11 · Summary
Key Takeaways
What every CVICU nurse, charge nurse, and house supervisor should walk away knowing from this case.
1
SAM is not just a HOCM diagnosis.

It can develop dynamically in patients with structural predisposition: concentric LVH, mitral annular calcification, posterior leaflet restriction, and age-related LV remodeling. No family history of HOCM required. This was a patient in his 70s post-CABG patient, not a classic obstructive cardiomyopathy presentation.

2
A hyperdynamic EF + hypotension = red flag for LVOTO, not cardiogenic shock.

The standard post-op reflex is to worry more as EF drops. Here, the EF went UP (>65%) after revascularization — and that was part of the problem. Hyperdynamic + underfilled + hypertrophied = SAM. The squeeze is not the issue. The cavity size and outflow geometry are.

3
Dynamic, reversible MR is the fingerprint of SAM.

Baseline MR was only trace. With hypovolemia/hypotension, it became severe. With volume + phenylephrine, it resolved. That reversibility — captured in real time on TEE — is the diagnostic key that distinguishes SAM-related MR from structural MR requiring surgical correction.

4
Phenylephrine is the right pressor. Inotropes are contraindicated.

Pure α₁ vasoconstriction raises afterload, slows LV emptying, and allows the cavity to fill more completely — mechanically opposing SAM. Dobutamine, milrinone, and epinephrine make it worse. "Shocking" a patient into a worse state with an inotrope is one of the most preventable catastrophes in cardiac surgery ICU care.

5
Volume is treatment, not just supportive care.

Fluid administration is an active pharmacological intervention for SAM. Expanding LV preload changes the geometry of the LVOT and directly reduces the Venturi force driving anterior leaflet motion. Cautious diuresis early must be weighed against the risk of re-triggering obstruction.

6
The anesthesiologist's intraoperative TEE changed this patient's course.

Without real-time TEE identification and documentation, the ICU team would have been flying blind. The note from the cardiologist gave the overnight team, the APRN, and the attending a clear framework: load-dependent dynamic SAM, resolves with volume/Neo, no inotropes. This is what astute echocardiographic recognition looks like in practice.

7
Know the cycle so you can break it before it spirals.

Hypovolemia → small LV → LVOT narrows → high-velocity flow → Venturi drag → SAM → severe MR → low forward output → hypotension → less preload → worse SAM. Every link in that chain is a target for intervention. Volume breaks the preload link. Phenylephrine breaks the afterload link. No inotropes keeps the contractility link from becoming lethal.
